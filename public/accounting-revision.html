1. The fundamentals of accounting

1.1 · Why accounting matters

Profit is not the same as cash.

Book-keeping records transactions systematically. Accounting classifies, summarises and interprets those records to help decisions. A business can earn profit on credit while lacking cash to pay suppliers. Records help an owner monitor performance, control resources and plan finance. A statement of profit or loss measures performance over a period; a statement of financial position shows resources and obligations on a date.

A trader sells goods costing $600 for $900 on credit. Gross profit is $300, but no cash has yet arrived. The customer owes $900.
Check: Goods costing $600 are sold on credit for $900. What is the immediate gross profit?
  1. $300
  2. $900
  3. $600
  4. $0

Answer A: Revenue $900 less cost of sales $600 gives gross profit $300. Credit terms affect collection, not this profit calculation.

Check: Which activity goes beyond book-keeping?
  1. Filing a receipt
  2. Interpreting profitability for a decision
  3. Recording an invoice
  4. Entering a bank payment

Answer B: Accounting includes interpretation and decision support, while book-keeping concentrates on systematic recording.

1.2 · The accounting equation

Assets = liabilities + owner's equity

Assets are resources controlled by a business, such as inventory, equipment and trade receivables. Liabilities are amounts owed, such as trade payables and loans. Owner's equity (capital) is the owner's claim after liabilities. Each transaction preserves the equation. Capital introduced increases equity; drawings reduce it; profit increases it and loss reduces it.

Assets $48,000 and liabilities $13,000 give equity $35,000. Paying a supplier $2,000 reduces both bank and payables; equity stays $35,000.
Check: Assets are $48,000 and liabilities $13,000. Calculate equity.
  1. $48,000
  2. $35,000
  3. $61,000
  4. $13,000

Answer B: Subtract liabilities from assets: 48,000 − 13,000 = 35,000.

Check: Equity is $18,000 and liabilities $7,000. Assets?
  1. $18,000
  2. $7,000
  3. $25,000
  4. $11,000

Answer C: Assets = equity + liabilities = 18,000 + 7,000.

1.1 · Study guide: The purpose of accounting

Book-keeping records every transaction accurately; accounting classifies, summarises and interprets those records so that the owner and other people can make decisions. The main reason for keeping accounts is to measure profit or loss for a period and to show the financial position at a date. Profit and cash are not the same thing, which is why both statements are needed.

What book-keeping is

Book-keeping is the recording part of accounting. Every time a business buys, sells, pays or receives something, a transaction happens. The book-keeper writes each transaction down in an organised way, using the figures shown on source documents such as invoices and receipts. The entries go first into books of prime entry and then into ledger accounts, either on paper or in a digital accounting system. The aim is simple: to keep a complete, accurate and permanent record of everything that has happened in money terms.

Book-keeping does not try to explain the figures. It does not decide whether a selling price is too low or whether a customer is slow to pay. It only makes sure that the facts are recorded correctly, in the right account, on the right side and with the right amount. If the recording is wrong, everything built on it later will also be wrong. That is why neat, systematic book-keeping is the foundation of the whole subject.

What accounting adds

Accounting starts where book-keeping stops. The accountant takes the recorded figures and classifies them into groups such as income, expenses, assets and liabilities. The figures are then summarised into financial statements, mainly the statement of profit or loss and the statement of financial position. Finally the accountant interprets the results. Is profit rising or falling? Can the business pay its debts on time? Which products earn the most?

A short way to remember the difference is that book-keeping records and accounting reports. The book-keeper asks what happened. The accountant asks what it means and what should be done next. In a very small business one person may do both jobs, but the two skills are still different. In the exam, if you are asked to explain the difference between them, give the purpose of each one, not just a list of tasks.

Why a business measures profit or loss

Profit is what is left when the expenses of a period are taken away from the income earned in the same period. A loss arises when expenses are greater than income. Measuring profit is the main reason most owners keep accounts. First, profit shows whether the business is worth running. An owner who could earn more in a paid job than the business earns may decide to close it. Second, the profit figure tells the owner how much can safely be taken as drawings. Taking out more than the profit reduces the capital invested. Third, other people need the figure: the tax authority uses it to calculate tax, and a bank uses it to judge whether a loan can be repaid.

Measuring a loss matters just as much. A loss reduces the owner's capital, and repeated losses can use it up completely. Knowing the size of the loss early gives the owner time to cut costs, change prices or stop an unprofitable activity.

Measuring profit also allows comparison. An owner can compare this year with last year, or one branch with another, and ask why the figures changed. Without a measured profit, every decision is a guess.

The two main financial statements

The statement of profit or loss covers a period of time, usually one year. It lists revenue, deducts cost of sales to find gross profit, and then deducts the other expenses to find profit for the year. Its heading always says for the year ended followed by a date.

The statement of financial position is a snapshot at one moment, usually the last day of the same year. It lists the assets the business owns, the liabilities it owes and the capital of the owner. Its heading says at followed by the date. Because it is a snapshot, the figures change the very next day when more transactions happen.

A common exam error is to mix up the headings. Remember that profit is earned over time, so the statement of profit or loss needs a period. Position is where the business stands now, so the statement of financial position needs a single date.

Profit is not the same as cash

Many students assume that a profitable business must have money in the bank. It often does not. Profit is measured when a sale is made, even if the customer has not yet paid. Cash is only received when the customer actually pays. A business that sells a lot on credit can show a healthy profit while its bank account is overdrawn.

Other transactions change the bank balance without touching profit at all. Buying a motor van reduces the bank, but the van is an asset, not an expense (only a part of its cost, called depreciation, is charged against profit each year, as you will see in Topic 4). Drawings reduce the bank, but they are the owner taking out capital, not a business expense. Receiving a loan increases the bank, but a loan is a liability, not income. Capital introduced by the owner increases the bank, but it is not profit.

This is why both statements are needed. The statement of profit or loss measures performance over the period. The bank balance in the statement of financial position shows how much money is available right now. A careful owner watches both figures.

Monitoring progress and making decisions

Accounting information turns a feeling about the business into facts. It has two uses. Monitoring progress means checking regularly how the business is doing against the past or against a target: is revenue up on last year, is the profit margin falling, are customers paying more slowly? Decision-making means choosing what to do next using those facts.

For pricing, knowing the cost of each item and the gross profit margin tells the owner whether a selling price covers costs. For expansion, a record of past profits and the present bank balance shows whether a second shop can be afforded or whether a loan is needed. For credit control, a list of trade receivables shows which customers owe money and for how long, so slow payers can be chased before cash runs out. For cost control, comparing this year's wages or electricity with last year's reveals costs that are growing faster than revenue.

People outside the business use the same information. A bank reads the statements before lending. A supplier checks them before giving credit. The tax authority uses them to calculate the tax due. Because so many decisions depend on them, the figures must be complete, accurate and consistent. The role of accounting is to provide reliable information for monitoring progress and for decision-making, not just to keep records.

From source document to decision

The accounting process is a chain, and each link depends on the one before it. A transaction happens and produces a source document, for example a sales invoice. The document is entered in a book of prime entry such as the sales journal. The totals are posted to ledger accounts. At the end of the period the ledger balances are listed in a trial balance to check the arithmetic. The financial statements are prepared from the trial balance after year-end adjustments. Finally the owner and other users read the statements and make decisions.

The first three links are book-keeping. The last two are accounting, and the trial balance sits between them. If a link breaks, for example an invoice is never recorded, revenue is understated, profit is understated, and a decision based on that profit could be wrong. Keeping the chain complete is the daily job of every business, however small.

1.2 · Study guide: The accounting equation

Everything a business owns (assets) was paid for either by the owner (capital, which the syllabus calls owner's equity) or by outsiders (liabilities), so assets always equal capital plus liabilities. Every transaction changes at least two items and leaves the equation balanced. The statement of financial position is this equation written out at one date, and capital changes only through capital introduced, profit or loss, and drawings.

Assets: what the business owns or controls

An asset is something of value that the business owns or controls and expects to benefit from in the future. Assets fall into two groups. Non-current assets are bought to be kept and used for more than one year, for example premises, machinery, fixtures and motor vehicles. They are not bought to be resold. Current assets are cash or items expected to turn into cash within one year, for example inventory, trade receivables, bank and cash in hand.

Trade receivables are the amounts owed to the business by customers who bought on credit. They are assets because the business has the right to receive that money. Inventory is goods bought for resale that are still unsold at the date in question.

In a statement of financial position, non-current assets are listed first, usually the most permanent first. Current assets follow, in order from the least liquid to the most liquid: inventory, trade receivables, bank, cash.

Liabilities: what the business owes

A liability is an amount the business owes to someone outside the business. It is a claim against the assets. Current liabilities must be paid within one year, for example trade payables (suppliers who sold goods to the business on credit), a bank overdraft and other short-term amounts owing. Non-current liabilities are not due within one year, for example a bank loan repayable in three years or a mortgage on the premises.

A bank overdraft needs care. When the business has money in the bank, the bank balance is an asset. When the business has spent more than it had and the bank has allowed the account to fall below zero, the overdraft is a liability. The same account can be either, depending on which way the balance points. In the exam, read the words before the figure: bank $1,200 is a current asset; bank overdraft $1,200 is a current liability.

Capital: the owner's claim

Capital is the amount the business owes to its owner. This sounds strange at first. The key idea is the business entity concept: for accounting purposes the business is treated as separate from the person who owns it. When Hana pays $10,000 of her savings into her shop's bank account, the shop has an asset of $10,000 in the bank and it owes Hana $10,000. That amount owed to the owner is called capital.

Capital changes in only four ways. It increases when the owner puts in more resources (capital introduced) and when the business makes a profit, because profit belongs to the owner. It decreases when the owner takes resources out for personal use (drawings) and when the business makes a loss. Transactions such as paying a supplier, buying a van or receiving a loan do not change capital at all; they only move value between assets and liabilities. Income and expenses do change capital, but only through the profit or loss they create.

The syllabus calls the owner's claim owner's equity; capital is the usual name for it in a sole trader's books. Its figure always equals net assets, which means total assets less total liabilities.

The equation and its three forms

The accounting equation states that assets equal capital plus liabilities. Written with the syllabus term it is assets equal liabilities plus owner's equity; the order of the two right-hand items does not matter. Everything the business owns was paid for either by the owner (capital) or by outsiders (liabilities). There is no third source of finance, which is why the two sides must always be equal.

The equation can be rearranged to find whichever figure is missing. If you know assets and liabilities, capital is assets less liabilities. If you know capital and liabilities, assets are the two added together. If you know assets and capital, liabilities are assets less capital. Learn the first form and the other two follow by simple algebra.

For example, a business with assets of $41,000 and liabilities of $9,500 has capital of $31,500. If a year later its assets are $44,000 and its capital is $33,200, its liabilities must be $10,800. Always write down the form of the equation you are using before you put the numbers in; the method mark depends on it.

Every transaction keeps the equation balanced

A transaction always changes at least two things, and after the change the equation still balances. This is the duality concept, and it is the reason double entry works. There are only a few patterns to learn.

Sometimes one asset goes up and another goes down by the same amount, so total assets do not change: buying a computer for cash, or a customer paying what they owe. Sometimes an asset and a liability rise together: buying goods on credit, or receiving a loan. Sometimes an asset and a liability fall together: paying a supplier, or repaying a loan. Sometimes an asset and capital move together: the owner introducing cash, the owner taking drawings, or the business earning income or paying an expense.

Income increases capital because it increases profit, which belongs to the owner. Expenses decrease capital for the same reason. When you analyse a transaction, name the two items, say whether each goes up or down, and check that the equation still holds.

The statement of financial position

The statement of financial position is the accounting equation written out in full at one date. It lists the assets, non-current first and then current. It then lists capital, followed by non-current liabilities and then current liabilities. The total of assets must equal the total of capital and liabilities because they are the two sides of the same equation.

The capital section usually shows how capital has moved during the year: opening capital, plus capital introduced, plus profit for the year (or less a loss), less drawings, giving closing capital. This small calculation links the statement of financial position to the statement of profit or loss, because the profit figure comes from there.

If the two totals do not agree, something has been left out or put in the wrong place. Do not force a balance by inventing a figure. Go back, re-add each section, and check that every item is an asset, a liability or part of capital.

Finding profit from the change in capital

Because capital only changes through capital introduced, profit or loss, and drawings, you can work backwards. If you know the capital at the start and at the end of a year, together with the drawings and any capital introduced, the profit is the only unknown. Profit equals closing capital less opening capital, plus drawings, less capital introduced.

Suppose capital was $15,000 at the start of the year and $17,400 at the end, drawings were $6,200 and no capital was introduced. The capital rose by $2,400 even though the owner took out $6,200, so the business must have earned $2,400 plus $6,200, which is $8,600. If the answer comes out negative, the business made a loss.

This method is used again in Topic 5 for incomplete records, so learn it well now. Set out the working line by line and label each line. A bare figure earns little if it is wrong, but labelled working can earn method marks even when one number slips.

2. Sources and recording of data

2.1 · Debit and credit entries

Every transaction has equal total debits and credits.

Debit increases assets, expenses and drawings. Credit increases liabilities, income and capital. Reverse these entries for decreases. Credit purchases of goods for resale debit purchases and credit the supplier; credit sales debit the customer and credit sales. Do not confuse the bank statement perspective with the business ledger perspective.

Buy goods for resale on credit from Noor for $750: debit purchases $750; credit Noor $750. Pay Noor later: debit Noor, credit bank.
Check: Which entry records goods for resale purchased on credit?
  1. Debit bank; credit sales
  2. Debit equipment; credit bank
  3. Debit purchases; credit supplier
  4. Debit supplier; credit purchases

Answer C: Purchases increase on the debit side, and the amount owed to the supplier increases on the credit side.

Check: A business receives a $4,000 bank loan. Entry?
  1. Debit loan; credit bank
  2. Debit purchases; credit loan
  3. Debit bank; credit revenue
  4. Debit bank; credit loan

Answer D: Bank assets and loan liabilities both increase.

2.1 · Ledger balances and running balances

Balance carried down closes a period; balance brought down opens the next.

A T account places debits on the left and credits on the right. Insert the difference on the smaller side to balance totals. Carry the balance forward on the opposite side. Asset and expense balances normally debit; liabilities, capital and income normally credit. A running-balance account updates the debit or credit balance after every transaction. Transfer revenue and expense balances to the statement of profit or loss; carry assets and liabilities into the next period.

Bank starts debit $1,200, receives $500 and pays $300. Closing debit balance = 1,200 + 500 − 300 = $1,400.
Check: Bank opens with debit $1,200, receives $500 and pays $300. Closing balance?
  1. $1,400 credit
  2. $2,000 debit
  3. $400 debit
  4. $1,400 debit

Answer D: An asset balance rises with receipts and falls with payments: 1,200 + 500 − 300 = 1,400 debit.

2.1 · Ledger divisions and digital records

Personal accounts support control; general accounts classify activity.

The sales ledger holds individual credit customers. The purchases ledger holds individual credit suppliers. The nominal or general ledger holds other accounts such as capital, expenses and assets. Digital ledgers can post automatically and produce balances quickly, but incorrect source data or mapping still produces incorrect records. Access controls, audit trails and reconciliations remain necessary.

A customer invoice updates that customer in the sales ledger and the sales account in the general ledger. A supplier invoice updates the purchases ledger.

2.1 · Posting a complete transaction sequence

Keep ledger sides consistent across the whole sequence.

Start by identifying accounts, then the nature of each account, then whether it increases or decreases. Post each entry to both accounts. When an expense account closes, transfer its debit balance to profit or loss. For a revenue account transfer its credit balance. Financial-position balances carry forward rather than being closed as expenses. A digital running-balance layout gives date, details, debit, credit and a balance after each transaction.

Owner invests $5,000: bank debit, capital credit. Buy equipment $1,200 cash: equipment debit, bank credit. Pay rent $300: rent debit, bank credit. Closing bank debit $3,500; equipment debit $1,200; rent debit $300; capital credit $5,000. Debits equal credits.

2.2 · Business source documents

Record from evidence; distinguish a transaction from a summary.

An invoice gives details of a sale or purchase on credit. A credit note reduces an invoiced amount, for example for returned goods. A debit note is sent by a buyer to a supplier with details of goods returned or an overcharge (asking for a credit note), or by a supplier to correct an undercharge; check who issued it and why. A receipt confirms payment; a cheque counterfoil records a cheque issued; a paying-in slip supports a bank deposit. A bank statement lists bank movements, while a statement of account summarises transactions and the balance with a customer. Documents can be paper or digital; keep retrievable records.

2.3 · Books of prime entry

Credit goods transactions use journals; cash movements use the cash book.

The sales journal records credit sales of goods; purchases journal records credit purchases of goods. Sales returns and purchases returns journals record the corresponding returns. Cash sales and cash purchases go in the cash book. The general journal records entries such as corrections, depreciation, opening entries and credit purchases of non-current assets. Journal totals post to general accounts, and individual invoices post to personal accounts.

Buy a delivery van on credit for $12,000. Use the general journal: debit motor vehicles and credit the supplier. It is not a purchase of resale goods.

  3. Petty cash book

2.3 · Cash book and discounts

Trade discount reduces invoice value; cash discount rewards prompt payment.

The cash book is both a prime-entry book and the ledger for bank and cash. Cash and bank receipts are debit entries; payments are credit entries. A transfer between cash and bank is a contra. An overdraft is a credit bank balance; physical cash cannot be overdrawn. Cheques, debit and credit card payments, online and bank transfers are recorded in the bank column; notes and coins in the cash column. Trade discount is deducted before recording; cash discount allowed is an expense and cash discount received is income.

List price $1,000 less 10% trade discount gives invoice $900. A 2% prompt-payment discount on $900 is $18, so cash paid is $882.
Check: A $1,000 list price has 10% trade discount then 2% cash discount. Payment?
  1. $882
  2. $880
  3. $900
  4. $980

Answer A: Invoice = 1,000 × 90% = 900. Payment = 900 × 98% = 882; the discounts do not simply add.

2.3 · Petty cash and recording choices

Imprest reimbursement restores the authorised float.

Petty cash pays small expenses supported by vouchers. Analyse expenditure into columns such as postage and travel. With an imprest, reimbursement equals the amount spent if there are no discrepancies. Keeping cash is convenient for small purchases but increases theft and handling risk. Manual books are inexpensive and usable without power but are slower to summarise. Digital entry speeds calculations and searching, but needs training, backups and checks against duplication.

An imprest of $150 has vouchers totalling $112. Cash remaining is $38; reimburse $112 to restore $150.
Check: An imprest is $150 and cash remaining is $38, with complete vouchers. Reimbursement?
  1. $188
  2. $112
  3. $38
  4. $150

Answer B: The reimbursement replaces expenditure: 150 − 38 = 112.

2.3 · Returns, discounts and cash-book posting

Record the net trade invoice first; recognise settlement discount when taken.

For credit sales, debit the customer and credit sales at invoice value after trade discount. A return debits sales returns and credits the customer. A cash discount allowed debits discount allowed and credits the customer as part of settlement; bank receives the cash amount. Reverse the perspective for purchases: returns and discount received reduce the supplier amount. A cash-book discount column is totalled and posted to the appropriate discount account, not balanced as a bank asset.

2.1 · Study guide: The double entry system of book-keeping

Double entry records every transaction twice, once as a debit and once as a credit, so the ledger always balances. This section gives you the rules for assets, liabilities, capital, income, expenses and drawings, shows you how to write up and balance a ledger account in T form and in three-column running balance form, how ledger accounts can be kept digitally, and how the ledger is divided into the sales ledger, the purchases ledger and the nominal (general) ledger.

Every transaction has two sides

Think about what really happens when a business buys a computer for $900 and pays by bank transfer. The business now owns a computer worth $900, so one asset has gone up. At the same time the money in the bank has gone down by $900. Nothing has been created or lost. Value has simply moved from one place to another. Accountants call this the duality concept. Every transaction has a receiving side and a giving side, and the two sides are always equal in money.

Double entry book-keeping is the system that records both sides. The account that receives value is debited and the account that gives value is credited. Because every debit has a matching credit, the total of all the debits in the ledger always equals the total of all the credits. This is what makes a trial balance possible later, and it is why a one-sided or missing entry can be discovered. If you record only one side, you have a list of events, not a set of accounts, and you cannot tell what the business owns or owes.

The debit and credit rules

Every account belongs to one of six groups: assets, expenses, drawings, liabilities, capital and income. The first three increase on the debit side. The last three increase on the credit side. A decrease is always recorded on the opposite side to an increase. Learn the table below until you can answer without thinking, because every later topic in the course depends on it.

Here is a way to make sense of the groups. Assets, expenses and drawings all represent value that the business holds or has used up, and all three normally have debit balances. Capital, liabilities and income all represent value that came into the business from somewhere, from the owner, from a lender or from customers, and all three normally have credit balances. When you meet a new transaction, ask three questions in order. Which two accounts are affected? What type of account is each one? Is each account increasing or decreasing? The answers tell you which account to debit and which to credit.

Two layouts: T form and three-column running balance

A ledger account in T form has the name of the account across the top, the debit side on the left and the credit side on the right. Each side has columns for the date, the details and the amount. The details column does not describe the transaction. It names the other account in the double entry, so that anyone reading the account can follow the entry to its partner. In the bank account a payment of rent is written as Rent on the credit side. In the rent account the same payment is written as Bank on the debit side.

The same account can be shown in the three-column running balance format. There is one list of entries in date order, with three money columns: debit, credit and balance. After every entry you work out the new balance and mark it Dr or Cr. Nothing is carried down, because the latest figure in the balance column is always the balance. Bank statements and computer ledgers use this layout. The debit and credit rules are exactly the same in both layouts. Goods for resale: purchases, sales and returns

Goods that a business buys in order to sell again are never recorded in an account called goods or inventory during the year. Four separate accounts are used instead. Purchases records goods bought for resale, at cost. Sales records goods sold to customers, at selling price. Purchases returns (returns outwards) records goods the business sends back to its suppliers. Sales returns (returns inwards) records goods that customers send back to the business.

We keep four accounts because the figures are needed separately when the statement of profit or loss is prepared, and because cost price and selling price are different amounts. Inventory is only counted and valued at the end of the period. Be careful with the word purchases. Buying a delivery van or a set of shelves is not purchases, because those items are not for resale. They are non-current assets and belong in their own asset accounts. Writing a van into the purchases account is an error of principle: expenses are overstated, profit is understated and the assets of the business are understated.

Balancing an account

At the end of a month or a year most accounts have entries on both sides, and the owner wants to know the net position. Balancing follows the same five steps every time. First, add up each side in rough. Second, find the difference between the two totals. Third, write the difference on the smaller side with the words Balance c/d and the last day of the period. Fourth, total both sides on the same line; the two totals are now equal. Fifth, bring the balance down on the opposite side with the words Balance b/d and the first day of the next period.

The balance b/d is the figure that tells you something. A debit balance b/d on a customer account means the customer still owes that amount. A credit balance b/d on the bank account means the business is overdrawn. If an account has entries on one side only there is nothing to balance; the total of that side is simply the balance. Income and expense accounts are not carried down at the year end. Their balances are transferred to the statement of profit or loss so they start the new year at zero, and the drawings account is closed by a transfer to the capital account. Only asset, liability and capital accounts carry a balance forward.

Dividing the ledger

A very small business may keep every account in one book. As the business grows, the ledger is split into three parts so that several people can work on it at the same time, each part is smaller and quicker to search, and an error can be traced to one part. The sales ledger holds one account for each credit customer. The purchases ledger holds one account for each credit supplier. The nominal ledger, also called the general ledger, holds every other account: non-current assets, expenses, income, capital, drawings, loans and the sales, purchases and returns accounts.

The cash book is treated as a separate book that contains the cash account and the bank account, so these two accounts are not written in the nominal ledger. Notice that the sales account is in the nominal ledger, not the sales ledger. The sales ledger contains only the people who owe money for sales. A common exam task gives a list of account names and asks which ledger each belongs to, so practise sorting them quickly and accurately.

Keeping ledger accounts digitally

Many businesses keep their ledger on a computer, using accounting software or a spreadsheet, instead of writing accounts by hand. The double entry rules do not change. What changes is who does the posting. The book-keeper enters each transaction once, from its source document, and chooses the accounts by name or code. The software then makes the debit and the equal credit automatically and updates the balance of every account at once. Accounts are usually shown in the three-column running balance format, so the balance after every entry is always visible.

Digital ledgers bring clear benefits. Posting is fast, the arithmetic is always right, balances are up to date at any moment, and a trial balance or a customer's statement can be printed in seconds. Records can be backed up and searched easily. There are limitations too. The computer posts exactly what it is given, so if the wrong account or amount is keyed in, the error goes straight into the ledger and the books may still balance. The business must pay for equipment and training, protect the data with passwords and backups, and plan for a power failure or a computer breakdown.

Reading an account: what the entries tell you

Examiners often give you a finished ledger account and ask what happened. Reading an account is a skill you can learn. Start with the heading: whose account is it, and in whose books? Then look at each entry and the name in the details column. In the account of a credit customer, debit entries are invoices for goods sold and credit entries are money received, discount allowed or goods returned. In the account of a supplier the picture is reversed: credit entries are invoices received, and debit entries are payments, discount received or goods sent back.

The balance b/d at the end tells you the position. On a supplier account a credit balance b/d is a current liability and appears in trade payables. On a customer account a debit balance b/d is a current asset and appears in trade receivables. On an expense account the debit total for the year is the amount charged in the statement of profit or loss. When a question says explain, do not simply name the entry. Say what it means for the business, for example that a customer paid $570 to settle a debt of $600 and was allowed $30 for paying promptly.

2.2 · Study guide: Business documents

Every entry in the books must be supported by a document that proves the transaction happened. This section explains the invoice, debit note, credit note, cheque counterfoil, paying-in slip, receipt, bank statement and statement of account: who issues each one, who receives it, what it is for, and how it is used as a source of information for the books. It also shows how trade discount and cash discount appear on these documents, and how documents can be produced and recorded manually or digitally.

Why documents come first

No entry should be made in the books from memory. Every transaction is recorded from a document that shows the date, the names of the two parties, what was supplied and how much is owed. These documents are the evidence that the figures in the accounts are real. They let the owner check the books, let an accountant verify them, and settle disputes when a customer says an invoice was never received or a supplier says a payment never arrived.

The flow usually starts with a purchase order from the buyer. When the goods are sent, the seller issues an invoice. If something is wrong with the goods, the buyer sends a debit note and the seller replies with a credit note. At the end of the month the seller sends a statement of account. The buyer then pays, by cheque, card or bank transfer, and may receive a receipt. Between the business and its bank there are three more documents: the cheque counterfoil, the paying-in slip and the bank statement. Each document is a source of information for the books of prime entry covered in section 2.3. Notice that the same document has a different name and a different use in the two businesses: the seller's sales invoice is the buyer's purchase invoice.

The invoice

The invoice is the most important document in credit trading. The seller prepares it when goods are supplied on credit and sends the top copy to the buyer, keeping a copy for its own records. It shows the invoice number and date, the names and addresses of both businesses, the quantity, description and unit price of each item, the total at list price, any trade discount deducted, the net amount payable and the terms of payment, for example 2 per cent cash discount if paid within 14 days.

The amount that matters for the books is the net amount after trade discount. The seller uses its copy to write up the sales journal and later debits the customer and credits sales. The buyer uses the invoice it receives to write up the purchases journal and then debits purchases and credits the supplier. The cash discount offered in the terms is only a possibility at this stage. It is not deducted on the invoice and it is only recorded if the buyer actually pays in time. Many invoices carry the letters E and OE, meaning errors and omissions excepted, which allow the seller to correct a mistake later.

Debit notes and credit notes

Sometimes the invoice turns out to be wrong. Goods may arrive damaged, the wrong items may be sent, or the buyer may be charged too much. The buyer then sends a debit note to the seller. This is a request: the buyer is saying that it intends to debit the seller's account, that is, to reduce what it owes, and it gives the reason and the amount. Usually no entry is made from the debit note, because the seller has not yet agreed. Some buyers record a return from their copy of the debit note instead, but a return must only ever be recorded once.

If the seller accepts the claim it issues a credit note, often printed in red to make it stand out from an invoice. The credit note confirms that the customer's account has been credited, which reduces the amount owed. Now entries are made. The seller records the credit note in the sales returns journal and then debits sales returns and credits the customer. The buyer records it in the purchases returns journal and then debits the supplier and credits purchases returns. The amount on a credit note is calculated at the same trade discount as the original invoice, so that the reduction matches what was charged.

The statement of account

At the end of each month the seller sends every credit customer a statement of account. It is a copy of the customer's account in the seller's sales ledger: the balance at the start of the month, each invoice added, each credit note and payment deducted, and the balance now owed. Its purposes are to remind the customer how much is due, to give the customer a chance to check the seller's record against its own purchases ledger, and to speed up payment.

A statement is not a source document for a new entry. The invoices and credit notes listed on it have already been recorded. If the buyer finds a difference, it is usually timing: a payment sent on the last day of the month, or an invoice posted after the statement was printed. Real errors, such as an invoice charged twice, are raised with the seller by a debit note or a phone call. The buyer normally pays the balance on the statement, less any cash discount still available, rather than paying each invoice separately.

Cheques, cheque counterfoils and receipts

A cheque is a written order from the drawer, the person who signs it, to the drawee, the drawer's bank, to pay a sum of money to the payee named on the cheque. When the cheque is written, the drawer fills in the cheque counterfoil, the stub left in the cheque book, with the date, the payee and the amount. The cheque goes to the payee, but the counterfoil stays with the drawer and is the source document for the payment in the bank column of the cash book. The payee pays the cheque into its own bank and records the receipt in its cash book.

A receipt is written proof that money has been received. It is most important for cash, because cash leaves no trace of its own. A shop gives a till receipt for a cash sale, and a card machine prints a receipt for a card payment. A business that pays a small cash expense asks for a receipt and attaches it to the petty cash voucher. For payments made through the bank, such as an online transfer, a separate receipt is often not issued, because the bank's confirmation and the bank statement are evidence enough. Receipts and counterfoils are source documents for the cash book and the petty cash book.

Paying-in slips and bank statements

When a business takes cash and cheques to the bank, it fills in a paying-in slip. The slip lists the notes and coins and each cheque, with the total paid in. The bank keeps the slip and stamps the counterfoil, which the business keeps. The stamped counterfoil proves the money reached the bank and is the source document for the debit in the bank column of the cash book. If the money came from the office cash, the same counterfoil supports a contra entry: debit bank, credit cash.

A bank statement is sent by the bank, on paper or online, usually every month. It is a copy of the business's account in the bank's records, so it is written from the bank's point of view: money paid in is shown as a credit and money paid out as a debit, the reverse of the business's own cash book. The business uses the statement as a source of information in two ways. It checks every receipt and payment against its cash book, and it finds items it did not know about, such as direct debits, standing orders, bank charges, interest and credit transfers from customers, which must then be entered in the cash book.

Trade discount and cash discount on documents

Trade discount is a reduction in the list price, often given to another trader or for a large order. It is shown on the invoice and deducted before the total is calculated. The ledger never shows trade discount: the sales journal, the purchases journal and the personal accounts all use the net figure. A credit note for returned goods must use the same percentage so that the customer is credited with exactly what was charged.

Cash discount is different. It is offered in the terms on the invoice, for example 2.5 per cent for payment within 10 days, but nothing is deducted until the customer pays in time. When that happens the seller records discount allowed, an expense, and the buyer records discount received, an income. Cash discount is calculated on the amount actually owed at the time of payment, that is the invoice total after trade discount and after any credit notes. A common mistake is to add the two percentages together; they are applied one after the other on different amounts.

Documents produced and recorded manually or digitally

Business documents can be produced by hand, on printed pads with carbon copies, or digitally, using a computer. A digital invoice or credit note is created on screen, sent to the customer by email or through an online system, and stored as a file. Many businesses now receive their bank statement online and make payments by online transfer instead of writing cheques. Whatever the form, the document does the same job: it is the evidence for the entry in the books.

The way the document is recorded can also be manual or digital. In a manual system a clerk copies each document into the correct book of prime entry by hand. In a digital system the details may flow straight into the accounting records when the document is created, for example a sales invoice that is entered in the sales journal and posted to the customer's account automatically. Digital documents are quick to send, cheap to store, easy to search and less likely to be lost or copied wrongly. On the other hand they depend on equipment and power, files can be deleted, corrupted or hacked unless they are backed up and protected, and a mistake keyed in once is repeated in every record that uses it. Paper documents need no equipment and give a physical record, but they are slower to prepare, take up storage space and can be lost or damaged.

2.3 · Study guide: Books of prime entry

Transactions are first listed in books of prime entry and only then posted to the ledger. This section covers the cash book with two and three columns, its dual function, discounts and contra entries, receipts and payments by cash, cheque, debit and credit card, online and bank transfer, the petty cash book with the imprest system, the sales, purchases and returns journals, and the general journal. It also weighs up keeping cash at the business premises and using manual or digital methods for the original entry of transactions.

Why transactions go through a book of prime entry

A busy business may have hundreds of invoices a week. If each one were written straight into the ledger, the sales account would be hundreds of lines long and several clerks would need the same page at the same time. A book of prime entry solves this. Transactions of one type are listed together in date order from their source documents, and only the totals are posted to the impersonal accounts. The individual items are posted to the personal accounts of customers and suppliers.

There are seven books of prime entry: the cash book, the petty cash book, the sales journal, the purchases journal, the sales returns journal, the purchases returns journal and the general journal. The journals are also called day books. Using them has three benefits. The ledger stays short and tidy. Work can be shared, with one person on each book. And the totals give useful information at once, such as credit sales for the month, without adding up the ledger. Remember that a book of prime entry is not part of the double entry itself; the debit and credit are made when the entries are posted. The one exception is the cash book, which is both a book of prime entry and the ledger account for cash and bank.

The journals for credit transactions in goods

The sales journal is written up from the copies of invoices sent to customers. Each line shows the date, the customer's name, the invoice number and the net amount after trade discount. Trade discount itself is never entered anywhere: there is no trade discount account. At the end of the period the journal is totalled. Each customer is debited with their own amount in the sales ledger, and the sales account in the nominal ledger is credited with the total. The purchases journal works the same way in reverse: each supplier is credited individually and the purchases account is debited with the total.

The two returns journals follow the same pattern with credit notes. From the sales returns journal, each customer is credited and the sales returns account is debited with the total. From the purchases returns journal, each supplier is debited and the purchases returns account is credited with the total. Only goods for resale go through these four journals. A machine bought on credit, or an old van sold on credit, goes through the general journal because it is not trading inventory. Cash sales and cash purchases never go in these journals; they go straight to the cash book.

The three-column cash book

The cash book has a debit side for money received and a credit side for money paid. Each side has a cash column and a bank column. This gives the cash book its dual function. It is a book of prime entry, because every receipt and payment is first recorded there from its source document. It is also part of the ledger, because the cash column and the bank column are the cash account and the bank account themselves. Only the other half of each entry needs to be posted.

A third column on each side records cash discount: discount allowed on the debit side, discount received on the credit side. Cash discount is entered only when payment is made within the agreed time, on the same side as the money. Work in order: list price less trade discount gives the invoice, less credit notes gives the amount owed, less cash discount gives the amount paid. The discount columns are memorandum columns, so they are totalled but never balanced. Discount allowed is posted to the debit of its account and discount received to the credit of its account. At the end of the period the cash and bank columns are balanced. Cash can only have a debit balance; the bank column may have a credit balance, which is an overdraft.

Contra entries and overdrafts

A contra entry is a transaction between the two columns of the cash book. When cash is paid into the bank, the bank column is debited and the cash column is credited on the same day. When cash is withdrawn from the bank for use in the office, the cash column is debited and the bank column is credited. Both halves of the double entry are inside the cash book, so nothing is posted to the ledger. The letter C is written beside both entries to show this. The source document for cash paid into the bank is the stamped counterfoil of the paying-in slip.

If the bank column is balanced and the credit side is larger, the balance c/d is written on the debit side and the balance b/d appears on the credit side. This credit balance is a bank overdraft, which is a current liability. It arises when the business has paid out more through the bank than it had in the account, with the bank's permission. During the next period, receipts reduce the overdraft and payments increase it. When a question gives an opening overdraft, write it as Balance b/d in the bank column on the credit side and work from there.

Cash, cheques, cards and transfers, and keeping cash on the premises

Customers can pay a business in several ways, and the business pays its suppliers and expenses in the same ways. The rule for the cash book is simple. Notes and coins go in the cash column. Everything that passes through the bank account goes in the bank column: cheques, debit and credit card payments, online transfers, bank transfers, standing orders and direct debits. Money received is a debit and money paid is a credit. A card payment from a customer is a bank receipt, because the card company pays the money into the business's bank account. A payment with the business's debit card leaves the bank at once. A purchase with a business credit card is paid from the bank when the card company's monthly bill is settled.

Because so much money now moves through the bank, many businesses keep only a little cash at their premises. Keeping some cash has benefits: change can be given to customers who pay in cash, small expenses can be paid at once, and money is still available if the card machine or the internet fails. It also has limitations: cash can be stolen or lost, mistakes are hard to trace, it earns no interest, insurance may not cover large amounts, and staff time is spent counting it and taking it to the bank. A sensible business keeps a small float and banks the rest every day.

The petty cash book and the imprest system

Small payments such as postage and bus fares are handled by a petty cashier in a petty cash book. Under the imprest system the cashier is given a fixed float, say $180, at the start of the period. Every payment is supported by a petty cash voucher signed by the person who received the money, with the receipt attached. At the end of the period the main cashier gives the petty cashier exactly the amount spent, so the float returns to $180. The purpose of the system is control. Cash in hand plus vouchers always equals the float, so a shortage is spotted at once; the cash at risk is limited to the float; and the petty cashier must account for every payment before receiving more money.

The petty cash book has a receipts column on the left and a total payments column on the right, followed by analysis columns, one for each type of expense, and a ledger column for small payments to suppliers. Each payment is written once in the total column and once in the correct analysis column. At the end of the period the analysis columns are totalled and cross-checked against the total column. Each expense total is posted to the debit of its expense account, and each item in the ledger column is debited to the supplier's personal account. The reimbursement is debited in the petty cash book and credited in the bank column of the main cash book.

The general journal

The general journal, often just called the journal, is the book of prime entry for anything that does not fit in the other six books. Its main uses are: opening entries when a business first keeps double entry records; the purchase or sale of a non-current asset on credit; the correction of errors; transfers between accounts, such as goods taken by the owner for private use or the year-end transfer of drawings to capital; and writing off an irrecoverable debt. Each entry shows the date, the account to be debited and the amount in the debit column, then the account to be credited, slightly indented, with the amount in the credit column, and finally a narrative, a short sentence explaining why the entry is made.

The narrative matters. The journal is the only book of prime entry where unusual entries are recorded, so a reader must be able to see the reason without searching elsewhere. In opening entries, every asset is debited and every liability is credited, and the difference is credited to capital. Journal entries are then posted to the ledger in the normal way. The journal itself is not an account, so it is never balanced.

Manual and digital methods of original entry

Original entry means the first recording of a transaction from its source document in a book of prime entry. It can be done manually, by writing in paper journals and a paper cash book, or digitally, by keying the document into accounting software or a spreadsheet, or by letting the system capture it automatically, for example when card receipts and transfers are downloaded from the bank.

Manual methods have benefits. They are cheap, need no equipment or special training, keep working when the power or the internet fails, and leave a written record that is hard to change without trace. Their limitations are that they are slow, every total must be added by hand so arithmetic errors are common, each item must be copied again when it is posted, and information such as the amount owed by a customer takes time to find.

Digital methods have benefits. Entries are fast, totals and postings are made automatically and the arithmetic is correct, the ledger is always up to date, and records are easy to back up and search. Their limitations are the cost of equipment, software and training, the risk of data loss, viruses or hacking, dependence on power and the internet, and the fact that a wrong account or amount keyed in is posted without question. Whichever method is used, every entry must still be supported by a source document.

3. Verification of accounting records

3.1 · Trial balance and its limitations

Equal debit and credit totals do not prove that every entry is correct.

A trial balance lists ledger debit and credit balances to test arithmetic agreement and to provide a summary from which financial statements are prepared. Asset, expense and drawings balances usually appear as debits; liabilities, capital and income as credits. It can reveal one-sided or unequal postings. To amend a trial balance that contains errors, move any balance shown on the wrong side, correct wrong amounts and re-total both columns. It cannot reveal complete omission, errors of original entry, commission or principle, complete reversal, or equal compensating errors. Prepare it from balances, not all ledger transaction totals.

A $300 sale is completely omitted. Both debit receivables and credit sales are missing; the trial balance still agrees.

3.1 · Recognising errors that balance

A balanced trial balance tests equality, not classification or completeness.

Omission leaves out both entries. Original-entry error uses the wrong amount in both accounts. Commission uses the wrong account of the same class, such as the wrong customer. Principle uses the wrong class, such as purchases instead of equipment. Complete reversal puts both entries on the opposite sides. Compensating errors cancel numerically. Explain the actual mistaken entries when identifying the type; a balanced total does not tell you which type occurred.

A $250 invoice to Salma is debited to Sara and credited correctly to sales. Both customers are receivable accounts: error of commission. Total receivables and total trial-balance debits remain unchanged.

  2. Principle
  3. Complete omission
  4. Complete reversal

Answer A: The wrong account of the same class was used.

3.2 · Correction journals and suspense

Use suspense only where an imbalance needs a temporary balancing entry.

Correct an error by removing the wrong effect and recording the required effect. A purchases entry posted to equipment is an error of principle and needs a transfer, not suspense. A one-sided entry may require suspense. If debit balances exceed credits, suspense initially has a credit balance. Calculate how a correction changes expenses, income, assets and profit rather than assuming every correction affects profit.

Repairs $400 were debited to equipment. Correct with debit repairs $400 and credit equipment $400. Profit and equipment each fall $400 before any related depreciation adjustment.

3.2 · Preparing a suspense account

Analyse the imbalance before choosing the suspense side.

If debit balances total less than credit balances, insert a debit suspense balance for the difference. Correct each error with a complete journal, using suspense only for the missing or incorrect balancing effect. An entry posted to the wrong side often requires twice the original amount to correct. A zero suspense balance confirms the identified imbalance is removed, but does not prove all balanced errors are corrected.

A $200 rent payment is correctly credited to bank but credited to rent instead of debited. Debits are $400 short. Suspense opens debit $400. Correct with debit rent $400 and credit suspense $400; this reverses the wrong credit and creates the required debit.

  3. $400
  4. $200

3.3 · Cash book update and bank reconciliation

Update the cash book first; reconcile timing differences second.

Use the bank statement to record bank charges, interest, direct debits, standing orders, transfers and cash-book errors not yet entered. Unpresented cheques and uncredited deposits are timing differences and do not need another cash-book entry. 3.1 · Study guide: The trial balance

A trial balance is a list of every ledger balance on one date, with debit balances in one column and credit balances in the other. If the two columns agree, the arithmetic of the double entry is probably correct. Agreement does not prove that every entry is right, because six types of error leave the totals equal.

What a trial balance is and why we prepare it

Every transaction is recorded twice: once as a debit and once as a credit, for the same amount. If every entry has been made correctly, the total of all the debit balances in the ledger must equal the total of all the credit balances. A trial balance tests this. It is simply a list of every account balance on one date, with the debit balances in one column and the credit balances in the other.

A trial balance has two main purposes. First, it checks the arithmetical accuracy of the double entry. If the columns do not agree, at least one error has been made and must be found. Second, it collects every balance in one place, which makes it the starting point for preparing the statement of profit or loss and the statement of financial position.

Remember that a trial balance is not an account and not a financial statement. It is a working list. It has no debit and credit sides in the ledger sense, only two columns of figures.

Which column? Deciding the side of each balance

The side of a balance follows directly from the double entry rules. Ask one question about each account: what kind of account is it? Assets, expenses, purchases and drawings are increased by debits, so they carry debit balances. Capital, liabilities, income and revenue are increased by credits, so they carry credit balances.

Returns are the opposite of the account they reverse. Sales returns reduce revenue, so they have a debit balance. Purchases returns reduce purchases, so they have a credit balance. Discounts follow the same logic: discount allowed is an expense (debit) and discount received is income (credit).

Two items catch students out. A bank overdraft is money owed to the bank, so it is a liability with a credit balance even though it sits in the cash book. Only the opening inventory appears in the trial balance, because the closing inventory has not yet been entered in the ledger when the trial balance is drawn up.

Preparing the trial balance step by step

Start with a heading that names the business and says Trial balance at, followed by the date. A trial balance is always at one date, never for a period. Then list the accounts in a sensible order. Many students use the order of the financial statements: revenue and expenses first, then assets, liabilities, capital and drawings. Any order is accepted as long as every balance appears once.

Write each balance in only one column. Use the rules in the table above. If you are unsure, think about the entries that built the balance. Rent received came from Dr Bank, Cr Rent received, so its balance is a credit.

Finally add both columns and write the totals on the same line. If they agree, rule them off. If they do not agree, do not force them. Find the difference, check the additions and the postings, and if the error is still hidden, enter the difference in a suspense account so that the totals agree for now.

Purposes and limitations of a trial balance

When the two totals agree, the double entry is arithmetically complete: for every debit there has been an equal credit. This is useful, but it is a limited test, and you must be able to explain its limitations.

First, the trial balance only compares totals. It cannot show whether an entry went to the right account, whether the amount was right in the first place, or whether a transaction was recorded at all. The six types of error explained below all stay hidden. Second, when the totals disagree, the trial balance shows that an error exists, but not where it is or how many errors there are. Third, it is not a financial statement. It does not show the profit for the year or the financial position, and it usually does not yet include closing inventory or year-end adjustments such as accruals, prepayments and depreciation for the year. Fourth, it is only as reliable as the balances copied into it: a balance missed out or copied wrongly gives a false result.

An agreed trial balance is therefore a good sign, not a guarantee. Businesses also use bank reconciliation statements (section 3.3) and control accounts (section 3.4) to check their records.

Amending a trial balance which contains errors

Examination questions often show a trial balance prepared by someone else that does not agree, and ask you to prepare a corrected version. Work through it line by line and ask three questions about each item.

Is it in the right column? Check every balance against the rules in the table above. Typical mistakes are returns, discounts, carriage and rent received placed on the wrong side, and capital or a loan shown as a debit.

Should it be there at all? Closing inventory is not a ledger balance on the trial balance date, so it is removed and shown as a note underneath. Each ledger balance appears once only.

Is the figure right? If the question says that a balance was copied wrongly, or that an error has been found and corrected in the ledger, use the corrected figure. For example, if rent paid by cheque of $90 was posted to the rent account as $900, the rent balance is reduced by $810.

Then add both columns again. If the corrected totals agree, any suspense account that was opened is no longer needed. Show a short working for each change so the examiner can follow it.

Errors that do make the trial balance disagree

Some errors break the equality of debits and credits, and the trial balance will catch them. The most common is a one-sided entry: an amount is entered in the cash book but never posted to the ledger account, or it is posted twice to one account. Another is posting to the wrong side, for example putting discount allowed on the credit side of its account. A wrong-side error creates a difference of twice the amount, because the debit column is short by the amount and the credit column is too large by the same amount.

Addition errors also show up. If the sales journal is overcast, the sales account receives a credit that is too large while the individual customer accounts are correct. If a ledger account is added wrongly, its balance is wrong on one side only. Finally, a balance copied into the wrong column of the trial balance, or left out of the trial balance altogether, makes the totals disagree even though the ledger itself is correct.

When the totals do not agree: the suspense account

If the totals still disagree after you have checked the additions and the balances, the accountant opens a suspense account for the difference. The suspense balance goes on the side that is smaller, so that the trial balance totals agree for the time being. If the credit column total is larger than the debit column total, the suspense account has a debit balance equal to the difference.

The suspense account is strictly temporary. It is a sign that something is wrong, not a solution. Each error is then found and corrected with a journal entry, and every correction that touches the suspense account reduces its balance. When all the errors have been found, the suspense account balance is nil. If financial statements have to be prepared before the errors are found, a remaining debit suspense balance is shown as a current asset and a remaining credit balance as a current liability. 3.2 · Study guide: Correction of errors

Errors are corrected with journal entries that remove the wrong effect and put in the right one. Errors that upset the agreement of the trial balance are corrected through a suspense account, which must end with a nil balance. Some errors change the profit for the year and the figures in the statement of financial position, so a statement of corrected profit is often needed.

Why corrections go through the journal

When an error is found, the book-keeper must not rub it out or cross it through. The correction is made with a new entry, and the general journal is the book of prime entry for it. The journal records the date, the account to be debited, the account to be credited, the amount and a narrative. The narrative matters: it explains what the error was and why the entry is being made, so that anyone checking the books later can follow what happened.

A correcting journal entry is still double entry. Every correction has a debit and an equal credit. If the original error affected only one side of the ledger, the other side of the correction goes to the suspense account. If the original error affected both sides equally, the correction moves amounts between the two accounts involved and the suspense account is not touched.

In the examination, write the journal in a simple table: the account debited first, the account credited underneath and slightly indented, then the narrative. Dates are given only when the question provides them.

The three-question method

Most mistakes in corrections happen because students jump straight to the answer. Instead, ask three questions in order. First, what entry was actually made? Write it down as a debit and a credit. Second, what entry should have been made? Write that down too. Third, what entry changes the first into the second? That third entry is the correction.

Take an example. A cheque of $90 paid for stationery was debited to the purchases account. What was done: Dr Purchases $90, Cr Bank $90. What should have been done: Dr Stationery $90, Cr Bank $90. The bank entry is right, so leave it alone. Purchases must lose $90 and stationery must gain $90. The correction is Dr Stationery $90, Cr Purchases $90.

This method works for every type of error, including reversals and wrong-side postings, where the correcting amount is not the same as the original amount. It also tells you immediately whether the suspense account is involved: if the entry that was made had a debit without a credit, or a credit without a debit, the suspense account fills the gap.

When the suspense account is needed

The suspense account is used only for errors that made the trial balance disagree. These are errors where the debits and credits recorded were not equal: a missing debit or credit, a posting to the wrong side, an addition error in one account or in a book of prime entry, or an amount posted twice to one account.

The suspense account is opened with the difference on the trial balance, on the side that makes the totals agree. If the debit column was smaller, suspense has a debit balance. Then, for each error, work out the correct entry to the real account and put the other side in the suspense account. For example, if discount allowed of $75 was entered in the cash book but never posted, the discount allowed account needs a debit of $75; the credit goes to suspense. After all errors are corrected the suspense account balances exactly to nil. If it does not, either an error has been missed or one of your corrections is wrong.

Never put errors of principle, commission, omission, original entry, complete reversal or compensating errors through suspense. They did not cause the difference, so they cannot remove it.

Entries on the wrong side: double the amount

A posting to the wrong side of an account is the trap that costs the most marks. Suppose rent received of $200 was debited to the rent received account instead of credited, and the cash book entry was correct. The rent received account now shows a debit of $200 where it should show a credit of $200. To correct it, the wrong debit must be cancelled ($200) and the correct credit made ($200). The correcting entry is therefore a credit of $400 to rent received, with a debit of $400 to suspense.

The trial balance difference confirms this. The wrong debit made the debit column $200 too large, and the missing credit made the credit column $200 too small. The difference was $400, exactly the correcting amount.

Contrast this with a missing entry. If the $200 had simply not been posted to rent received at all, the correction would be a single credit of $200, because nothing needs cancelling. Read the wording carefully: entered on the debit side means wrong side (double), while not posted or omitted from the account means one-sided (single).

Effect of errors on profit

Many errors change the profit for the year because they overstate or understate income or expenses. Once an error is corrected, the profit must be recalculated. The rule is simple. If the error made an expense too high or income too low, profit was understated, so the correction increases profit. If the error made an expense too low or income too high, profit was overstated, so the correction reduces profit.

Some errors do not change profit at all. Moving an amount between two expense accounts (commission) leaves total expenses the same. Moving an amount between two income accounts does the same for income. Errors in asset and liability accounts only, such as a receipt posted to the wrong customer, do not touch profit either. An amount moved between carriage inwards and carriage outwards changes gross profit without changing profit for the year, because carriage inwards is part of cost of sales while carriage outwards is an expense below gross profit, but both are deducted before profit for the year. In the same way, income posted to revenue instead of another income account changes gross profit but not profit for the year.

Statement of corrected profit and the statement of financial position

A statement of corrected profit starts with the draft profit for the year. Underneath, list each error with the amount by which the correction increases or decreases profit, and write no effect where that is the case. Add the increases, deduct the decreases and show the corrected profit for the year. Keep the layout clear: one line per error, numbered as in the question.

Errors also affect the statement of financial position. A non-current asset wrongly debited to an expense means non-current assets were understated. A missing credit to a customer account means trade receivables were overstated. A payment not posted to a supplier means trade payables were overstated. Because profit flows into capital, every error that changes profit also changes closing capital by the same amount. Drawings posted to an expense account understate drawings and understate profit by the same figure, so closing capital is unchanged even though two lines were wrong.

If a suspense balance remains after the known corrections, show it in the statement of financial position: a debit balance as a current asset, a credit balance as a current liability. This should be temporary.

3.3 · Study guide: Bank reconciliation

The bank balance in the cash book and the balance on the bank statement are rarely the same on a given date. Some items reach the bank before the business records them, and some items reach the cash book before the bank processes them. A bank reconciliation first updates the cash book for items the business did not know about, and then prepares a statement that explains the remaining difference by timing.

Two records of the same money

A business keeps its own record of its bank account in the bank columns of the cash book. The bank keeps a separate record in its own books and sends a copy as a bank statement. Both records describe the same account, so in theory they should agree. In practice they usually differ on any particular day.

There are two reasons. First, timing. The business writes a cheque and records it at once, but the supplier may take days to pay it in. The business pays cash into the bank late on the last day of the month, but the bank processes it the next day. Second, information. The bank takes charges, pays standing orders and receives transfers from customers without telling the business until the statement arrives. Occasionally one side has simply made an error.

Remember whose point of view each record takes. In the cash book, money in the bank is an asset, a debit balance. On the bank statement, the same money is a liability of the bank to its customer, so it appears as a credit balance. A healthy bank statement balance is therefore shown as a credit, and an overdraft as a debit.

Items on the bank statement but not in the cash book

When the statement arrives, the book-keeper ticks every item that also appears in the cash book. The unticked items on the statement are things the business did not yet know about. They are real transactions and must be entered in the cash book now. The table lists the common ones and shows the cash book entry each needs.

A dishonoured cheque needs special care. When a customer's cheque was received, the business debited bank and credited the customer. If the bank later refuses the cheque, the money never arrived, so the entry is reversed: credit bank and debit the customer. The customer owes the money again.

A bank error, for example a cheque belonging to another customer charged to this account, is not entered in the cash book, because it is not a transaction of the business. It is shown in the reconciliation statement and reported to the bank for correction.

Step one: update the cash book

Start from the cash book bank balance before any adjustment. Bring it down on the correct side: a debit balance if the business has money at the bank, a credit balance if it is overdrawn. Then enter each item found on the bank statement that was missing from the cash book. Receipts (credit transfers, interest received) go on the debit side. Payments (charges, interest charged, standing orders, direct debits, dishonoured cheques) go on the credit side. Correct any cash book errors at the same time: if a payment was understated, enter the extra amount as a payment; if a receipt was understated, enter the extra amount as a receipt.

Balance the account. The new balance c/d is the updated cash book balance. This is the true bank balance of the business and it is the figure that appears in the statement of financial position, as a current asset if it is a debit balance or as a current liability (bank overdraft) if it is a credit balance.

In the examination this is usually presented as a short bank account in T form or as a column of adjustments. Both are accepted, but the entries must be on the correct sides and the final balance must be labelled.

Step two: the bank reconciliation statement

The statement explains why the updated cash book balance still differs from the bank statement balance. It is a list, not an account. Give it a heading with the business name and the date.

The usual layout starts with the balance on the bank statement. Add the uncredited deposits, because the bank will add them soon. Deduct the unpresented cheques, because the bank will take them soon. Adjust for any bank error in the direction that undoes the error. The result must equal the updated cash book balance. If it does not, something is missing or on the wrong side.

You can also start from the updated cash book balance and work towards the bank statement balance. Then the directions reverse: add unpresented cheques and deduct uncredited deposits. Always write which balance you start from and which you finish at, so the examiner can follow the logic.

Overdrafts: work with signs

An overdraft turns the balances negative, and students often reverse the adjustments. The safest method is to treat an overdraft as a negative number and keep the same rules. In the cash book update, payments make the balance more negative and receipts make it less negative. In the reconciliation statement, starting from a bank statement overdraft, still add uncredited deposits and still deduct unpresented cheques; the arithmetic takes care of the signs.

For example, a bank statement overdraft of $1,010 with an uncredited deposit of $180 and unpresented cheques of $655 gives minus 1,010 plus 180 minus 655 = minus 1,485. If the bank also wrongly charged $60 that belongs to another customer, add that back: minus 1,485 plus 60 = minus 1,425, which should be the updated cash book overdraft.

Label every overdraft clearly, in both the cash book (balance c/d on the debit side, b/d on the credit side) and the statement (overdraft or in brackets). The updated overdraft is shown in the statement of financial position as a current liability.

Why businesses prepare bank reconciliations

A bank reconciliation is a control. It checks the accuracy of the cash book against an independent record, the bank's own books. It brings into the cash book items the business did not know about, so that the bank balance in the financial statements is correct. It finds errors made by the business, such as a transposed figure, and errors made by the bank, which can be reported and corrected. It reveals dishonoured cheques quickly, so the customer can be contacted and credit control tightened. It can also discourage fraud, because a person taking money from the account would be found when the records are compared.

For these reasons reconciliations are prepared regularly, often monthly, and in many businesses they are done by someone who does not write up the cash book. In the examination, a question on uses or benefits usually expects three or four of these points, each explained in a sentence rather than listed as single words.

Digital transactions and the bank reconciliation

Most businesses now pay and receive money digitally, by card, online transfer, direct debit or mobile payment. This changes the reconciliation in four ways.

Fewer timing differences. Online transfers usually reach the account on the same day, so there are fewer unpresented cheques and uncredited deposits. However, card takings may reach the account a day or two after the sale, often after the card company has deducted its fee. The fee must be entered in the cash book, and any takings not yet received are a timing difference.

More items to record from the statement. Many payments and receipts are started by the bank or by other people, such as direct debits, card fees and customer transfers. The business often learns of them only from the bank statement, so updating the cash book is still essential.

Faster and more frequent checks. Online banking lets the business see its statement at any time, and accounting software can import bank transactions and suggest matches with cash book entries. Reconciliation can be done daily or weekly instead of monthly.

New risks. An imported item can be matched to the wrong entry or recorded twice, a payment can be sent to the wrong account, and online fraud can go unnoticed. A person must still review every unmatched item, so the reconciliation remains an important control.

3.4 · Study guide: Control accounts

A control account is a summary account for a whole ledger. The sales ledger control account summarises every customer account and the purchases ledger control account summarises every supplier account. Its entries come from the totals in the books of prime entry, so its balance should equal the total of the individual balances in that ledger. Control accounts help to find errors, prevent fraud and give quick totals for trade receivables and trade payables.

Why a business keeps control accounts

A business with hundreds of customers has hundreds of accounts in its sales ledger. Adding them all up every time the owner wants to know the total trade receivables is slow, and an error in any one account is hard to find. A control account solves this. It is one account that records, in total, everything that was posted to the individual accounts. Credit sales are entered as one monthly total from the sales journal, receipts as one total from the cash book, and so on.

If every individual posting was made correctly, the balance on the control account must equal the sum of the individual balances in the ledger. When the two do not agree, there is an error somewhere in that ledger, and the search is limited to one ledger rather than the whole system.

Control accounts also give instant totals for trade receivables and trade payables for the trial balance and the statement of financial position, they make fraud harder because the person keeping the individual accounts is usually not the person keeping the control account, and they help a business with incomplete records to work out missing figures such as credit sales.

Where the figures come from

Every entry in a control account is a total taken from a book of prime entry. This is the key idea: the control account never uses individual invoices or receipts, only the period totals. Credit sales come from the sales journal total, sales returns from the sales returns journal total, receipts from customers from the bank and cash columns of the cash book, and discount allowed from the discount allowed column of the cash book. Irrecoverable debts written off, contras, interest charged and dishonoured cheques come from the general journal (and the cash book for dishonoured cheques).

The same applies to the purchases ledger control account, using the purchases journal, purchases returns journal, cash book payments and discount received column, and the general journal. Cash sales and cash purchases never appear, because they do not pass through the sales or purchases ledger at all. The table shows the source for each item.

The sales ledger control account

The sales ledger control account behaves exactly like one large customer account. Anything that increases what customers owe is debited; anything that reduces it is credited. Debits: opening debit balances, credit sales, dishonoured cheques (the debt comes back), interest charged on overdue accounts, and refunds paid to customers who had overpaid. Credits: receipts from customers, discount allowed, sales returns, irrecoverable debts written off, contras with the purchases ledger, and opening credit balances.

At the end of the period the account is balanced. The debit balance carried down is the total trade receivables. If some customers have credit balances at the end of the period, they are shown separately as a credit balance carried down on the debit side of the account, with the main debit balance on the credit side. Both balances are then brought down on their own sides.

The layout matters. Write the date or month, a clear description for every entry (for example Sales journal, Cash book, Discount allowed, Irrecoverable debts, Purchases ledger contra), and total both sides on the same line.

The purchases ledger control account

The purchases ledger control account is the mirror image. It behaves like one large supplier account, so increases in what the business owes are credits and reductions are debits. Credits: opening credit balances, credit purchases, interest charged by suppliers on overdue accounts, and refunds received from suppliers who had been overpaid. Debits: payments to suppliers, discount received, purchases returns, contras with the sales ledger, and opening debit balances.

The credit balance carried down is the total trade payables. Any debit balances in the purchases ledger at the period end, where a supplier owes the business, are shown as a separate debit balance carried down on the credit side.

Watch for distractors. Cash purchases, carriage, and the purchase of non-current assets on credit from a supplier who is not a trade supplier do not go through the purchases ledger control account. In the same way, an allowance for irrecoverable debts is never entered in the sales ledger control account, because it is a general estimate and does not change what any particular customer owes.

Contra entries: when a customer is also a supplier

Sometimes the same person buys from the business and sells to it. For example, a printer may buy paper from a stationer and also print the stationer's catalogues. The printer then has an account in the stationer's sales ledger (as a customer) and an account in the purchases ledger (as a supplier). Instead of each paying the other in full, they agree to set one debt against the other and settle only the difference.

The set-off is called a contra. It is recorded in the general journal: debit the purchases ledger control account and credit the sales ledger control account with the smaller of the two balances. The same entry is made in the two individual accounts. Both the amount owed to the supplier and the amount owed by the customer fall by the amount of the contra.

Suppose the business owes supplier Z $820 and Z owes the business $350. The contra is $350. After it, the business owes Z $470 and Z owes nothing. In the examination, name the entry as Purchases ledger contra on the credit side of the sales ledger control account and Sales ledger contra on the debit side of the purchases ledger control account.

Credit balances in the sales ledger and debit balances in the purchases ledger

Normally customers owe the business, so sales ledger accounts have debit balances. A credit balance appears when the business owes the customer. This can happen when a customer overpays, pays an invoice twice, pays in full and then returns goods, or takes a cash discount that was credited after payment. The business may refund the customer (debit the control account, credit bank) or allow the credit against the next invoice.

In the purchases ledger the opposite can occur: a supplier owes the business, giving a debit balance, when the business overpays or returns goods after paying in full.

In the control account these unusual balances are shown separately rather than netted off. In the statement of financial position, credit balances in the sales ledger are not deducted from trade receivables; they are shown as a current liability (other payables). Debit balances in the purchases ledger are shown as a current asset (other receivables). This keeps assets and liabilities from being understated.

Using control accounts to find missing figures

Because a control account is complete for the period, any one missing figure can be found by balancing it. If a trader has not kept a sales journal but knows the opening and closing trade receivables, the receipts, discount allowed and returns, credit sales is the figure that makes the control account balance. Enter everything you know, total the larger side, and the gap on the other side is the missing figure.

For example, opening receivables $6,200, closing receivables $7,150, receipts $48,900, discount allowed $1,050, sales returns $800 and irrecoverable debts $300. Credit side: 48,900 + 1,050 + 800 + 300 + closing balance 7,150 = 58,200. Debit side: 6,200 + credit sales. Credit sales = 58,200 minus 6,200 = $52,000. The same method finds credit purchases from a purchases ledger control account. This technique is used again in incomplete records (section 5.6).

Limitations, and the impact of digital recording

Control accounts are useful but limited. Some errors affect the control account and the individual accounts equally, so they stay hidden: a transaction omitted from a book of prime entry, an error of original entry, or an invoice posted to the wrong customer (error of commission). Control accounts are therefore used together with the trial balance and the bank reconciliation. At IGCSE you prepare control accounts and explain their uses; you are not asked to reconcile a control account balance with a list of individual ledger balances.

Most businesses now record credit sales, purchases, receipts and payments digitally. When an invoice is entered once, accounting software posts it to the customer's account and to the control account at the same time. Totals for trade receivables and trade payables are then available at any moment, posting and addition errors largely disappear, and no time is spent preparing control accounts by hand. Card receipts and online payments can be matched to customer and supplier accounts automatically.

However, a mistake made when data is entered, such as a wrong amount or the wrong customer, flows into both records, so the control account cannot reveal it. Access to the system must be controlled to prevent fraud, data must be backed up, and staff must be trained to review the figures the software produces.

4. Accounting procedures

4.1 · Capital and revenue items

Capital expenditure creates or improves a non-current asset; routine running costs are revenue.

The cost of an asset includes expenditure needed to bring it into intended use, such as installation. Routine repairs, fuel and insurance are expenses. Capital receipts include owner investment, loans and asset-sale proceeds; revenue receipts arise from normal income. Treating an expense as an asset overstates assets and profit; treating an asset as an expense understates both before depreciation effects. Classify by substance, not the size of payment.

Machine $5,000 plus installation $500 has capital cost $5,500. A later service costing $200 is an expense.
Check: Machine $5,000, installation $500 and routine service $200. Initial capital cost?
  1. $5,500
  2. $5,700
  3. $5,000
  4. $500

Answer A: Installation is needed to put the machine into use. Routine servicing is a revenue expense.

Check: Routine repair costs incorrectly capitalised cause, before depreciation effects:
  1. Profit lower and assets higher
  2. No effect on either
  3. Profit and assets to be overstated
  4. Profit and assets to be understated

Answer C: The expense is omitted and an asset wrongly increased.

4.1 · Capital receipts and revenue receipts

Receiving cash does not necessarily create revenue.

Revenue receipts arise from trading or other income, such as service fees. Capital receipts include new owner capital, loans and proceeds from selling non-current assets. A loan increases cash and liabilities without increasing profit. An asset disposal may create a gain or loss, but its entire proceeds are not trading sales. Distinguish improving capacity from maintaining existing capacity when deciding whether spending creates an asset.

A business receives $10,000 from a loan and $2,000 from service fees. Cash increases $12,000, but income from these receipts is $2,000.
Check: A new bank loan $10,000 is received. Immediate profit effect?
  1. $20,000 increase
  2. $0 increase
  3. $10,000 increase
  4. $10,000 decrease

Answer B: The loan is a liability, not revenue.

4.2 · Depreciation methods

Depreciation spreads the cost of a non-current asset over its useful life; the carrying amount is not a market value.

Depreciation is the loss in value of a non-current asset through use, wear and tear, passage of time or obsolescence; charging it matches the cost to the periods that benefit. Straight-line depreciation uses cost less expected residual value divided by useful life, or a stated percentage of cost. Reducing balance applies a rate to carrying amount. Revaluation depreciation for suitable small assets such as loose tools uses opening value plus additions less closing valuation. Journal: debit depreciation (expense) and credit the provision for depreciation account; under the revaluation method the asset account is credited directly. Choose a method reflecting use: an even service pattern may fit straight line; loose tools may fit revaluation. Depreciation reduces profit without a current cash payment.

Equipment $10,000, residual value $1,000, life 3 years: annual straight-line charge $3,000. At 20% reducing balance with no residual calculation, first-year charge $2,000; second $1,600.
Check: Cost $10,000, residual $1,000, useful life 3 years. Annual straight-line depreciation?
  1. $2,000
  2. $9,000
  3. $3,000
  4. $3,333.33

Answer C: (10,000 − 1,000) ÷ 3 = 3,000.

Check: Opening tools $900, purchases $300, closing tools valuation $800. Revaluation depreciation?
  1. $200
  2. $1,200
  3. $100
  4. $400

Answer D: 900 + 300 − 800 = 400.

4.2 · Asset purchases and disposal accounts

Disposal result = proceeds − carrying amount at disposal.

Record a purchase by debiting the asset cost account and crediting bank or the supplier (general journal if on credit). On disposal debit the disposal account and credit the asset account with cost; debit the provision for depreciation account and credit disposal with the accumulated depreciation. Credit disposal with proceeds and debit bank. Transfer the remaining balance to profit or loss: proceeds above carrying amount produce profit, and proceeds below it produce loss. Charge any depreciation required up to disposal under the stated policy.

Cost $8,000, accumulated depreciation $5,000 and sale proceeds $2,500. Carrying amount $3,000, so loss $500.
Check: An asset cost $8,000; accumulated depreciation $5,000; proceeds $2,500. Result?
  1. $500 profit
  2. $5,500 loss
  3. $2,500 profit
  4. $500 loss

Answer D: Carrying amount = 8,000 − 5,000 = 3,000. Proceeds are 500 below carrying amount.

4.2 · Recording disposal in three ledger accounts

Remove both the historic cost and its accumulated depreciation.

The three accounts are the non-current asset account, the provision for depreciation account and the disposal account. Debit disposal and credit the asset account with original cost. Debit the provision for depreciation account and credit disposal with the accumulated depreciation. Debit bank and credit disposal for sale proceeds. If disposal debits exceed credits there is a loss: debit profit or loss and credit disposal. If credits exceed debits there is a gain: debit disposal and credit profit or loss. Remove the disposed asset from future depreciation calculations.

Cost $6,000; depreciation $4,500; proceeds $1,800. Disposal debit $6,000; credits $4,500 and $1,800 = $6,300. Debit disposal $300 and credit profit or loss $300 to close the gain.
Check: Cost $6,000, accumulated depreciation $4,500, proceeds $1,800. Disposal result?
  1. $300 gain
  2. $300 loss
  3. $1,800 gain
  4. $4,200 loss

Answer A: Carrying amount = 1,500; proceeds exceed it by 300.

4.3 · Accrued and prepaid expenses

Expense = cash paid + closing accrual − opening accrual + opening prepayment − closing prepayment.

Charge the expense belonging to the period, regardless of payment date. A closing accrual is a current liability (other payables) and adds to the expense. A closing prepayment is a current asset (other receivables) and reduces the expense. In the expense ledger account, a closing accrual is a balance carried down on the debit side (brought down as a credit); a closing prepayment is carried down on the credit side (brought down as a debit). Journal: accrual, debit expense and credit other payables; prepayment, debit other receivables and credit expense. Reverse or bring forward the balances into the next period consistently.

Rent paid $12,000 includes $2,000 for next year and there is no opening prepayment or accrual. This year expense is $10,000; current asset $2,000.
Check: Rent paid $12,000 includes $2,000 for next year, with no opening adjustment. Expense?
  1. $2,000
  2. $10,000
  3. $14,000
  4. $12,000

Answer B: Exclude the future-period amount: 12,000 − 2,000 = 10,000.

Check: Insurance expense $1,200 is unpaid at year-end. It is:
  1. An expense of this year and a current liability (other payables)
  2. A prepayment (other receivables)
  3. Drawings
  4. Capital expenditure

Answer A: The cost belongs to this year (matching), with the amount still payable shown as an accrual in other payables.

4.3 · Accrued and prepaid income

Income = cash received + closing receivable − opening receivable + opening income in advance − closing income in advance.

Earned income not yet received (accrued income) is a current asset, shown in other receivables. Cash received before earning the income (income in advance) is a current liability, shown in other payables. For accrued income, carry down a balance on the credit side of the income account (brought down as a debit); for income received in advance, carry down a balance on the debit side (brought down as a credit). Journal: accrued income, debit other receivables and credit income; income in advance, debit income and credit other payables. The same matching concept applies to rent received, commission and interest.

Commission received $3,000 and another $400 earned but not received, with no opening balances: income $3,400 and income receivable $400.
Check: Commission received $3,000; $400 earned remains unpaid; no opening balances. Income?
  1. $3,000
  2. $400
  3. $3,400
  4. $2,600

Answer C: Add income earned but not yet collected: 3,000 + 400 = 3,400.

4.3 · Adjustments with opening balances

Opening balances prevent this year payments being mistaken for this year expense.

For expenses, subtract an opening accrual because its payment belongs to last year. Add an opening prepayment because that amount now belongs to this year. Reverse those directions for corresponding income adjustments. Show a working table with opening amount, cash paid or received, closing amount and the matched annual total. The closing adjustment also appears as an asset or liability in financial position.

Electricity cash paid $2,500 includes last year accrual $200. Current year unpaid $350. Expense = 2,500 − 200 + 350 = $2,650.
Check: Electricity paid $2,500; opening accrual $200; closing accrual $350. Annual expense?

  2. $2,350
  3. $2,500
  4. $2,650

Answer D: Deduct the old liability and add the new one: 2,500 − 200 + 350 = 2,650.

4.4 · Irrecoverable debts and recovery

Write off a specific uncollectable amount before calculating an allowance.

An irrecoverable debt is no longer expected to be collected. Debit irrecoverable-debt expense and credit the customer. If money is later recovered, first reinstate the debt (debit the customer, credit irrecoverable debts recovered, which is income), then record the receipt (debit bank, credit the customer), so the customer record shows the debt was eventually paid. Do not leave a known uncollectable balance among current assets.

Receivables $10,000 include a debt of $600 that cannot be collected. Write off $600; remaining receivables $9,400. A later recovery $200 increases bank and income.
Check: Receivables $10,000 include an irrecoverable $600. Receivables before a general allowance?
  1. $9,400
  2. $10,600
  3. $10,000
  4. $600

Answer A: The specific debt is removed first: 10,000 − 600 = 9,400.

Check: An allowance falls from $800 to $500. Effect on profit, other things equal?
  1. $800 increase
  2. $300 increase
  3. $300 decrease
  4. $500 decrease

Answer B: The reduction reverses part of the previous estimated expense.

4.4 · Allowance for irrecoverable debts

Expense the change in allowance, not the full closing allowance every year.

An allowance estimates losses on remaining receivables. Deduct it from gross receivables in the statement of financial position. Creation or increase: debit the statement of profit or loss (expense) and credit the allowance for irrecoverable debts account. A decrease: debit the allowance account and credit the statement of profit or loss (added to profit as income). Calculate it after specific write-offs. This applies prudence: it recognises collection uncertainty without identifying which customers will default. Older textbooks may use a different name for this estimate; in 0452 answers always write allowance for irrecoverable debts.

After write-offs, receivables $20,000 need a 5% allowance: $1,000. Existing allowance $700 means an increase charged to profit of $300; net receivables $19,000.
Check: Receivables $20,000; required allowance 5%; existing allowance $700. Additional expense?

  2. $300
  3. $1,000

4.4 · Recovering a debt previously written off

A recovery is not a new sale.

Where a debt has already been removed, record the recovery without restoring sales revenue. A clear two-step method reinstates the customer by debiting the customer and crediting irrecoverable debts recovered, then debits bank and credits the customer for the payment. The net effect is debit bank and credit recovery income. Do not leave the recovered amount as an outstanding receivable.

A debt written off last year is recovered for $240. Net journal: bank debit $240 and recovery income credit $240.

4.5 · Inventory valuation and profit effects

Use the lower of cost and net realisable value for each inventory item.

Cost includes expenditure to bring inventory to its present location and condition. Net realisable value is expected selling price less completion and selling costs. Compare item by item, not by offsetting gains on one item against losses on another. Closing inventory reduces cost of sales and is a current asset. Overstated closing inventory overstates gross profit, profit, assets and equity; the opening error affects the following period in the opposite direction.

Item A costs $500 and can sell for $460 with selling costs $20. NRV $440 is lower, so record $440 and recognise a $60 reduction.

  2. $800 understated
  3. $400 overstated
  4. $400 understated

Answer C: Closing inventory is deducted in cost of sales; overstating it understates cost of sales.

4.5 · Inventory item-by-item comparison

Apply the lower value separately to each item.

For each inventory line calculate cost and NRV; choose the lower, then total chosen amounts. Do not compare only total cost with total NRV because one item gain must not offset another item loss. Opening inventory errors affect the cost of sales of this year; closing inventory errors affect gross profit, profit for the year, current assets and equity of this year, and then the opening inventory of the next year. Understatement has the opposite effect from overstatement.

4.1 · Study guide: Capital and revenue expenditure and receipts

Capital expenditure buys or improves non-current assets and is shown in the statement of financial position. Revenue expenditure is the day-to-day cost of running the business and is charged to the statement of profit or loss. Capital receipts (capital introduced, loans, proceeds from selling non-current assets) are not income, while revenue receipts (sales, rent received, commission received) are. Classifying an item wrongly changes both the profit for the year and the asset total.

Two kinds of spending and why the difference matters

Every payment a business makes is either capital expenditure or revenue expenditure. Capital expenditure buys something that will help the business for several years: a van, a computer, a shop extension. The benefit lasts, so the cost is recorded as a non-current asset in the statement of financial position and then spread over the years of use through depreciation. Revenue expenditure buys something that is used up quickly: wages for this month, electricity for this quarter, fuel for this week. The benefit is gone by the end of the year, so the whole cost is charged to the statement of profit or loss of that year.

The difference matters because the two kinds of spending go to two different statements. If you put a cost in the wrong statement, the profit for the year is wrong and the asset total is wrong at the same time. The owner may think the business earned more or less than it really did, and may take drawings or ask for a loan based on a wrong figure. The test is the nature of the spending, not its size: a $40,000 payment for inventory is still revenue expenditure, and a $600 payment for a printer is still capital expenditure.

What goes into the cost of a non-current asset

The capital cost of a non-current asset is not only the price on the invoice. It includes every cost that is needed to get the asset to the place where it will be used and to make it ready to work. For a machine this means delivery, a concrete base, wiring and installation, and testing before the first use. For a building it includes legal fees and any alterations needed before it can be occupied. For a vehicle it includes sign-writing or a delivery box fitted when the vehicle is bought.

Costs that happen after the asset is working are revenue expenditure: fuel, insurance, road tax, servicing, repairs and the wages of the operator. Staff training is also revenue expenditure, because the knowledge belongs to the person and not to the asset. The table shows typical items. Receipts are classified in the same way as payments. A revenue receipt is income earned from the normal activities of the business. For a shop this is sales; for a tutoring centre it is fees; for any business it also includes rent received, commission received and interest received. Revenue receipts are credited to income accounts and appear in the statement of profit or loss.

A capital receipt does not come from trading. The three common examples are capital introduced by the owner, a loan received, and the money received when a non-current asset is sold. None of these is income. Capital introduced increases the capital account. A loan increases a liability. The proceeds of selling an asset go to the disposal account, and only the difference between the proceeds and the carrying amount is a profit or loss on disposal. A business that credits a $10,000 loan to sales will show a profit that is $10,000 too high and will leave a $10,000 liability out of the statement of financial position.

Difficult cases: repairs, improvements and small items

Most items are easy to classify, but three cases need care. First, repairs against improvements. Replacing a broken window is a repair: the building is no better than before, so it is revenue expenditure. Replacing single glass with double glazing that cuts heating costs is an improvement, so it is capital expenditure. A builder's invoice often mixes both, and you must split it.

Second, a business may use its own workers and materials to build an asset, for example a workshop building its own storage racks. The wages and materials used are capital expenditure, because they created an asset. They are moved out of the expense accounts and into the asset account.

Third, materiality. A stapler may last five years, but recording a $9 stapler as a non-current asset and depreciating it would be a waste of effort and would not change any decision. Small items like this are treated as revenue expenditure. The business should decide a limit and apply it consistently.

The effect of classification errors on the financial statements

A classification error always hits both statements. If revenue expenditure is treated as capital expenditure, an expense is missing from the statement of profit or loss, so profit is overstated. The same amount has been added to a non-current asset, so assets are overstated, and because profit is added to capital, capital is overstated too. If capital expenditure is treated as revenue expenditure, everything is the other way round: profit, non-current assets and capital are all understated.

Receipts behave in the same way. A capital receipt treated as income overstates profit. A revenue receipt treated as a capital receipt understates profit. The table summarises the four cases. If the wrong entry is in the capital account itself, for example capital introduced credited to sales, closing capital is correct in total but the profit for the year inside it is still wrong. There is also a second effect in later years: an asset that was wrongly expensed is never depreciated, so profits in future years are overstated a little, and an expense that was wrongly capitalised is depreciated, so future profits are understated a little. In the exam you are usually told to ignore depreciation when you calculate the effect, unless the question says otherwise.

The accounting concepts behind the rule

The capital and revenue distinction is an application of the matching concept. An asset that will be used for five years helps to earn income in each of those five years, so its cost should be matched against the income of all five years through depreciation, not charged entirely to the first year. Charging the whole cost at once would make year one look terrible and years two to five look better than they really were.

Prudence also plays a part. If there is real doubt whether a payment has created a lasting benefit, it is safer to treat it as an expense, so that profit and assets are not overstated. Materiality allows small long-lived items to be expensed. Consistency requires the business to use the same classification rules every year, so that profits of different years can be compared fairly. When a question asks you to explain a classification, name the concept and say what would go wrong if it were ignored.

A three-question check for any item

When you meet an item in a question, ask three questions in order. One: is this a receipt or a payment? Two: if it is a payment, will the benefit last more than one year? If yes, it is probably capital expenditure, unless it is too small to matter. If no, it is revenue expenditure. Three: if it is a receipt, did the business earn it by trading or by providing a service? If yes, it is a revenue receipt. If the money came from the owner, a lender or the sale of an asset, it is a capital receipt.

Then decide where the item goes. Capital expenditure is debited to a non-current asset account. Revenue expenditure is debited to an expense account. A revenue receipt is credited to an income account. A capital receipt is credited to capital, to a loan account or to the disposal account. Write the correct account name in your answer, not just the words capital or revenue, because the double entry is where the marks are.

4.2 · Study guide: Accounting for depreciation and disposal of non-current assets

Depreciation spreads the cost of a non-current asset over the years it is used, so that each year's profit carries a fair share of the cost. The straight-line, reducing balance and revaluation methods each suit different assets. The charge is debited to the statement of profit or loss and credited to a provision for depreciation account, and the asset appears in the statement of financial position at cost less the provision. When an asset is sold or part-exchanged, a disposal account compares the proceeds with the carrying amount to find the profit or loss on disposal.

What depreciation is and why it is charged

A non-current asset is bought to be used for several years. It loses value as it is used. Depreciation is the way the accountant charges that loss of value as an expense, a part at a time, over the years the asset is used. It applies the matching concept: each year that benefits from the asset should bear a share of its cost. It also applies prudence: if no depreciation were charged, the asset would stay at its original cost in the statement of financial position for ever, and profit would be overstated.

There are four causes. Wear and tear: machinery and vehicles wear out with use. Passage of time: a lease on a building runs out whether or not the building is used. Obsolescence: a computer still works but is out of date and cannot run new software. Depletion: a quarry or mine has less material left each year. Land is normally not depreciated because it does not wear out.

Two things depreciation is not. It is not a cash payment: no money leaves the business when depreciation is charged. It is not a valuation: the carrying amount is cost less depreciation to date, and it may be very different from the price the asset would sell for.

The straight-line method

The straight-line method charges the same amount every year. The formula is cost less residual value, divided by the expected useful life in years. Sometimes a question gives a percentage instead: 20 per cent per annum on cost means one fifth of the cost every year, so the asset is written off over five years.

For example, a delivery bicycle costs $1,800, is expected to last four years and to be sold for $200 at the end. The annual charge is ($1,800 less $200) divided by 4 = $400. After one year the carrying amount is $1,400; after two years $1,000; after four years $200, the residual value.

This method suits assets that give roughly the same service every year, such as office furniture, fixtures and leases. It is simple and easy to check. Its weakness is that many assets lose more value when they are new, and repair costs rise as the asset ages, so the total cost of using the asset (depreciation plus repairs) rises over time instead of staying level.

The reducing balance method

The reducing balance method charges a fixed percentage of the carrying amount at the start of each year, not of the original cost. Because the carrying amount falls every year, the charge also falls. The asset is never fully written off, so a small balance always remains.

For example, a laptop costs $2,000 and is depreciated at 40 per cent per annum reducing balance. Year 1: 40 per cent of $2,000 = $800, carrying amount $1,200. Year 2: 40 per cent of $1,200 = $480, carrying amount $720. Year 3: 40 per cent of $720 = $288, carrying amount $432. Notice that in year 2 you multiply by $1,200, not by $2,000. Using cost every year is the most common mistake and turns reducing balance into straight-line.

This method suits assets that lose most of their value early and need more repairs later, such as motor vehicles and computers. High depreciation plus low repairs in early years, and low depreciation plus high repairs in later years, gives a fairly even total cost of using the asset each year.

The revaluation method

Some assets are too small and too numerous to depreciate one by one: loose tools in a garage, crockery in a restaurant, small containers in a laboratory. For these the business uses the revaluation method. At the end of each year the items are valued. Depreciation for the year is the opening valuation, plus the cost of any items bought during the year, less the closing valuation.

For example, a garage valued its loose tools at $1,250 on 1 January 2026, bought tools costing $400 during the year, and valued them at $1,100 on 31 December 2026. Depreciation = $1,250 + $400 less $1,100 = $550. In the ledger the loose tools account is debited with the opening balance and the purchases, credited with $550 transferred to the statement of profit or loss, and the balance carried down is the closing valuation of $1,100.

The method is practical, but the result depends on the judgement of the person who values the items, so it is less objective than the other two methods.

Whichever method is chosen, the total depreciation over the whole life of the asset is the same: cost less what the asset is eventually sold for. The methods only change which years carry the charge.

Typical choices are straight-line for buildings, fixtures, furniture and leases; reducing balance for motor vehicles, computers and machinery; and revaluation for loose tools, crockery and similar small items. Land is normally not depreciated.

Two accounts are kept for each class of asset. The asset account records the asset at cost and stays at cost until the asset is disposed of. When an asset is sold, both its cost and its accumulated depreciation must be removed from the books, and the result of the sale must be found. The disposal account does this in four steps. The effect of depreciation and disposals on the financial statements

Depreciation reduces profit for the year and reduces the carrying amount of non-current assets by the same amount. Because profit is added to capital, capital also falls by the charge. Nothing happens to the bank balance. If a business forgets to charge depreciation, profit, non-current assets and capital are all overstated.

A profit on disposal is added to gross profit as other income; a loss on disposal is listed with the expenses. After the disposal the asset's cost and its provision both disappear from the statement of financial position. The quick way to find the result of a disposal is: proceeds less carrying amount at the date of sale. A positive answer is a profit, a negative answer is a loss. 4.3 · Study guide: Other payables and other receivables

The statement of profit or loss must show the expenses and income that belong to the year, not the cash that happened to be paid or received. An accrued expense is owed at the year end and is added to the expense; a prepaid expense has been paid for next year and is deducted. Income works the same way: income still owed to the business is added, and income received in advance is deducted. The closing accruals and prepayments then appear in the statement of financial position as other payables and other receivables.

Expense for the year is not cash paid

A business pays its bills when they arrive, not neatly at the year end. Some bills for this year are still unpaid on the last day; some payments made this year cover next year. If the statement of profit or loss simply listed the cash paid, the profit would depend on the timing of payments rather than on the real cost of running the business. The matching concept (also called the accruals concept) says that the expense charged must be the amount used up in the year, and that the income shown must be the amount earned in the year.

This gives us four adjustments. An accrued expense is an amount used up but not yet paid; it is added to the cash paid. A prepaid expense is an amount paid but not yet used up; it is deducted. Accrued income is earned but not received; it is added to cash received. Income received in advance is received but not yet earned; it is deducted. Every one of these also creates a balance in the statement of financial position, because at the year end someone owes someone.

Accrued expenses: adding what is still owed

Suppose Karim's shop pays electricity quarterly in arrears. By 31 December 2026 he has paid $2,800 for the first three quarters. The fourth quarter, estimated at $950, will be paid in January 2027. The electricity used in 2026 is $2,800 + $950 = $3,750, and that is the expense in the statement of profit or loss. The $950 is a current liability at 31 December 2026, shown under other payables.

In the electricity account, the payments are on the debit side. At the year end the account is closed by crediting it with the transfer of $3,750 to the statement of profit or loss. To make the account balance, $950 is entered on the debit side as balance c/d, and it appears below the line as a credit balance b/d. A credit balance on an expense account always means an accrual, because the business owes that amount. When the bill is paid in January, the payment is debited and cancels the credit balance, so the $950 is never charged twice.

Prepaid expenses: taking out what belongs to next year

Now suppose Karim pays his shop insurance of $1,800 on 1 October 2026 for the twelve months to 30 September 2027. Only three months, October to December, belong to 2026: $1,800 x 3/12 = $450. The other nine months, $1,350, belong to 2027. If there was no opening prepayment, the insurance expense for 2026 is $450, and the $1,350 is a current asset at 31 December 2026, shown under other receivables.

In the insurance account the payment of $1,800 is a debit. The transfer to the statement of profit or loss of $450 is a credit. The balance c/d of $1,350 is entered on the credit side and brought down as a debit balance. A debit balance on an expense account means a prepayment: the business has paid for something it has not yet used, which is an asset. Next year that $1,350 b/d is the first debit in the account and becomes part of the 2027 expense.

Stationery or packaging materials still unused at the year end are treated in the same way as a prepayment: deduct the cost of the unused stationery from the expense and show it as a current asset.

Income owing and income received in advance

Income accounts follow the same logic but the sides are reversed, because income is a credit. Suppose Karim sublets a storeroom for $300 a month. If the tenant has paid only $3,300 by 31 December 2026, one month ($300) is still owed. Rent receivable for the year is $3,600, and the $300 owed is accrued income, a current asset under other receivables. In the rent receivable account the receipts are credits, the transfer to the statement of profit or loss of $3,600 is a debit, and the balance c/d of $300 is entered on the credit side and brought down as a debit balance: an asset.

If instead the tenant had paid $3,900, the extra $300 is January's rent received in advance. Rent receivable for 2026 is still $3,600, and the $300 is a current liability under other payables. The balance c/d of $300 goes on the debit side and is brought down as a credit balance. A quick memory aid: a debit balance brought down is always an asset (prepaid expense or accrued income); a credit balance brought down is always a liability (accrued expense or income received in advance).

Follow the same five steps for any expense or income account. One: write the opening balance b/d on the correct side. 4.4 · Study guide: Irrecoverable debts and allowance for irrecoverable debts

When a credit customer will definitely not pay, the debt is irrecoverable and is written off as an expense, removing it from trade receivables. When some customers might not pay but nobody knows which, the business creates an allowance for irrecoverable debts, usually a percentage of the remaining trade receivables. Each year the allowance is adjusted to the required closing figure and only the increase or decrease goes to the statement of profit or loss. Money later received for a debt already written off is income, not a new sale.

Why debts go bad and what to do when they do

A business that sells on credit takes a risk. Most customers pay, but some do not: a customer goes bankrupt, closes down, moves away without leaving an address, or simply refuses to pay a disputed invoice. Once the business is certain that a debt will never be received, the amount is called an irrecoverable debt. It must be written off, because a trade receivable that will never turn into cash is not an asset.

The double entry is: debit irrecoverable debts account (an expense), credit the customer's account in the sales ledger. The customer's balance falls to zero, or to the part that will still be paid if only some of the debt is lost. At the year end the irrecoverable debts account is transferred to the statement of profit or loss as an expense. The sale that created the debt stays in sales: the goods were delivered and revenue was earned. What has gone wrong is the collection, and that is a cost of doing business on credit.

If a debt is written off and the trade receivables figure in the question is given before the write-off, you must deduct the debt before doing anything else, including calculating an allowance.

Recovering a debt that was written off

Sometimes a customer whose debt was written off pays after all, perhaps the next year. The receipt is not a new sale and it does not go back into the customer's balance as if nothing had happened. It is income, called irrecoverable debts recovered.

The neat way to record it is in two steps. First, reinstate the debt: debit the customer's account, credit irrecoverable debts recovered. This leaves a record in the customer's account that the debt was once written off and then paid, which is useful if the customer asks for credit again. Second, record the receipt: debit bank (or cash), credit the customer's account. The customer's balance is zero again. If a question asks only for the net effect, it is debit bank and credit irrecoverable debts recovered.

At the year end the irrecoverable debts recovered account is transferred to the statement of profit or loss as other income, added after gross profit. Some businesses net it against irrecoverable debts expense instead; either is acceptable if it is clear.

Why an allowance for irrecoverable debts is needed

At the year end a business usually has trade receivables that are not yet irrecoverable but may become so. It cannot write them off, because it does not know which customers will fail. But it also should not show trade receivables at their full value, because experience says some of that money will not arrive. The answer is an allowance for irrecoverable debts: an estimate of the amount of trade receivables that will probably not be collected.

Two concepts support this. Prudence says do not overstate assets or profit; the allowance reduces trade receivables to the amount that is realistically expected. Matching says the cost of a bad debt belongs in the year the sale was made, not the later year when the customer finally fails; the allowance charges an estimate of that cost in the year of sale.

In 0452 the allowance is normally a percentage of trade receivables at the year end, after irrecoverable debts have been written off. For example, trade receivables of $42,000 after write-offs and a policy of 5 per cent give a required allowance of $2,100. The percentage is based on past experience of how many customers fail.

Creating, increasing and decreasing the allowance

In the first year the allowance is created: debit statement of profit or loss, credit allowance for irrecoverable debts account, with the full required amount. In later years the allowance account already has a balance. The business calculates the required closing allowance and compares it with the existing balance. Only the difference is recorded. If the required figure is higher, the increase is debited to the statement of profit or loss and credited to the allowance account. If the required figure is lower, the decrease is debited to the allowance account and credited to the statement of profit or loss as income.

This is the point students most often get wrong. Suppose the allowance is $1,200 at the start of the year and $1,500 is required at the end. The expense for the year is $300, not $1,500. The $1,200 was already charged in earlier years. Charging the full $1,500 again would count the same expected loss twice and understate profit. The table sets out the three cases. The allowance account is a credit balance that is carried forward year after year. Presentation in the financial statements

In the statement of profit or loss there can be up to three lines. Irrecoverable debts written off are an expense. An increase in the allowance for irrecoverable debts is an expense. A decrease in the allowance is income, shown after gross profit with other income such as rent received. Irrecoverable debts recovered are also income.

In the statement of financial position the allowance is never shown as a liability. It is deducted from trade receivables under current assets: trade receivables $36,000, less allowance for irrecoverable debts $1,800, net $34,200. Irrecoverable or doubtful? Deciding and explaining

Exam questions often give a list of situations and ask what to do. A customer declared bankrupt with nothing left to pay the people it owes: irrecoverable, write off the whole debt. A bankrupt customer whose trustee will pay 20 cents in the dollar: write off 80 per cent, keep 20 per cent in trade receivables. A customer who is three months late and not answering letters: doubtful; keep the debt but make sure the allowance covers it. A customer who disputes part of an invoice because goods were damaged: that is not a bad debt but a reason for a credit note.

When you explain your treatment, use three sentences: what the situation is, what the entry is, and what the effect on the statements is. For example: the debt of $640 is irrecoverable because the customer has died with no estate; it is written off by debiting irrecoverable debts and crediting the customer; profit falls by $640 and trade receivables fall by $640. Short, specific answers like this score full marks.

4.5 · Study guide: Valuation of inventory

Closing inventory is deducted in cost of sales and shown as a current asset, so its value changes gross profit, profit for the year and the statement of financial position all at once. Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value, item by item, so that no profit is recorded before goods are sold and no loss is hidden. An error in closing inventory affects this year's profit and reverses in the next year, because this year's closing inventory is next year's opening inventory.

Why the inventory figure matters so much

Closing inventory appears in two places. In the statement of profit or loss it is deducted from opening inventory plus purchases to find cost of sales. In the statement of financial position it is a current asset. One figure, two statements. Put the figure $1,000 too high and cost of sales is $1,000 too low, gross profit and profit for the year are $1,000 too high, current assets are $1,000 too high and, because profit flows into capital, equity (the owner's capital) is $1,000 too high.

The inventory figure is also one of the easiest to get wrong, because it comes from a physical count and from valuing hundreds of different items, not from the double entry system. That is why accountants have a clear rule for valuing it and why examiners test the rule so often. Businesses count inventory at the year end, list every item with its quantity, and then put a value on each line. The rest of this section is about how that value is chosen.

What cost includes

Cost means all the expenditure needed to bring the goods to their present location and condition. For a retailer this is the purchase price after deducting trade discount, plus carriage inwards, import duties and any other cost of getting the goods into the shop. For a manufacturer it also includes the production costs of turning raw materials into finished goods. Cost does not include the costs of selling the goods, such as carriage outwards, advertising or the wages of sales staff, and it does not include a share of the office expenses.

For example, a shop buys 200 lamps at a list price of $20 each with a trade discount of 10 per cent, and pays $150 carriage inwards and $250 import duty. The cost is $4,000 less $400 = $3,600, plus $150 plus $250 = $4,000, which is $20 per lamp. If 30 lamps are unsold at the year end, their cost is $600. Always work out the cost per unit when a question gives quantities, because the comparison with net realisable value is usually made per unit.

What net realisable value means

Net realisable value (NRV) is what the business expects to get from selling the goods, after paying any further costs to finish them and to sell them. It is the expected selling price less costs to complete less selling costs. For goods in good condition the NRV is usually well above cost, because the business sells at a profit. NRV falls below cost when goods are damaged, out of fashion, out of date, or when the market price has dropped.

For example, a dress cost $60 and would normally sell for $110. It has a torn seam. It can be repaired for $15 and then sold for $70. NRV = $70 less $15 = $55. Because $55 is lower than the cost of $60, the dress is valued at $55 and the business recognises a loss of $5 now, before the dress is sold. If instead the dress could be sold for $90 after the $15 repair, NRV would be $75, which is above cost, and the dress would stay at $60.

Applying the rule item by item

Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value for each item or each group of similar items separately. You must not compare total cost with total NRV, because the extra value on items that will sell at a profit would cancel out the loss on items that will sell below cost, and the loss would be hidden. The table shows the method with four items. Total cost is $2,700 and total NRV is $2,860, so comparing totals would give $2,700. Comparing item by item gives $2,460, which is lower, because item B and item D are both written down to their NRV and items A and C stay at cost.

The procedure for any question is the same. Make a table with columns for cost, NRV and the lower figure. Fill in NRV only after deducting repair and selling costs. Choose the lower figure for each line. Add the lower column. That total is closing inventory.

The concepts behind the rule

Three concepts explain why inventory is valued in this way. Prudence: a loss that is expected should be recognised at once, so goods that will sell for less than they cost are written down now, but a gain that has not yet happened is not recorded, so goods are never valued above cost. Realisation: profit is earned when goods are sold, not when they are bought or when they sit on the shelf, so valuing inventory at selling price would record profit before the sale. Consistency: the business must use the same method of valuing inventory each year, so that the profits of different years can be compared and the owner cannot move profit from one year to another by changing the method.

A common exam question asks why inventory is not valued at selling price. The answer combines realisation and prudence: the sale has not happened, the goods might never sell at that price, and recording the profit now would overstate both profit and current assets.

The effect of inventory errors on profit, this year and next

Because closing inventory is deducted in cost of sales, overstating it understates cost of sales and overstates gross profit and profit for the year by the same amount. The inventory figure in current assets is overstated, so assets are valued too high, and because the profit is added to the owner's capital, equity is overstated by the same amount. Understating closing inventory has the opposite effect. The table sets out both cases.

The error does not stop there. This year's closing inventory becomes next year's opening inventory. Opening inventory is added in cost of sales, so if it is too high, next year's cost of sales is too high and next year's profit is too low by the same amount. Over the two years the errors cancel, and the total profit for the two years is correct, but each year on its own is wrong. For example, if closing inventory at 31 December 2026 is overstated by $900, profit for 2026 is $900 too high and profit for 2027 is $900 too low. The statement of financial position at 31 December 2027 is correct, because by then the overstated goods have gone, but the one at 31 December 2026 was wrong.

Common problems in the inventory count

Questions often describe mistakes made during the count and ask you to correct the figure. Goods valued at selling price must be converted back to cost: if the mark-up is 25 per cent, cost is selling price divided by 1.25. Goods left out of the count, perhaps stored in a van or a second storeroom, must be added at cost. Damaged or obsolete goods counted at cost must be reduced to their NRV. Goods that belong to a customer and are only being stored, or goods already sold but not yet collected, must be taken out. Goods bought and received before the year end must be included even if the invoice has not yet arrived.

Work through each item separately and write the adjustment with a plus or minus sign before you touch the total. Then recalculate gross profit if the question asks for it. Remember that a change in closing inventory changes gross profit by exactly the same amount in the same direction: inventory up $300 means gross profit up $300.

5.1 · Sole trader profit or loss

Cost of sales = opening inventory + net purchases + carriage inwards − closing inventory.

A sole trader owns and controls the business, keeps profits and normally has unlimited liability. Trading businesses sell goods; service businesses earn fees; manufacturers produce goods; one business can combine these activities. For a trader calculate net revenue after sales returns, then gross profit, add other income and subtract period expenses. Service statements normally start with fee income. Use a dated heading and reconcile net assets to owner funds.

5.3 · Company finance and limited liability

Equity is shareholder finance; a debenture is borrowing.

A company is legally separate from its owners. Limited liability normally limits shareholder loss to their investment and unpaid share commitments. Incorporation can improve access to capital but adds reporting and administration. Ordinary shares carry ownership and voting rights with variable dividends and are repaid last if the company is wound up; preference shares normally receive a fixed-rate dividend paid before any ordinary dividend. Debentures (loan capital) are long-term loans with a fixed interest charge that must be paid whether or not there is a profit, and are repayable on a stated date. Issued capital is allocated to shareholders; called-up capital is the amount requested; paid-up capital is the amount actually paid. Companies may trade, provide services or manufacture.

5.1 · Study guide: Sole traders

A sole trader prepares two main financial statements at the end of each year. The statement of profit or loss measures the profit earned over the period, and the statement of financial position shows what the business owns and owes on the last day. Every year-end adjustment from Topic 4 must be built into both statements.

Being a sole trader: advantages and disadvantages

A sole trader is a business owned by one person. The owner may employ staff, but takes all the decisions, provides the capital and keeps all the profit. A sole trader can be a trading business (buying and selling goods), a service business (selling skill or time), a manufacturing business (making the goods it sells, see 5.4) or a combination of these, such as a baker who makes bread and also runs a delivery service.

The main advantages are that the business is easy and cheap to set up, the owner keeps all the profit, decisions can be made quickly, and the accounts are private. The main disadvantages are unlimited liability, which means the owner's private possessions can be taken to pay the business's debts, limited capital because it comes from one person and what that person can borrow, heavy workload with nobody to share it, and no continuity: the business may end when the owner is ill or dies.

In an advise question, always weigh both sides and finish with a clear recommendation for the owner in the scenario.

Two statements, two different questions

At the end of every financial year a sole trader wants answers to two questions. How much profit did I make this year? What does my business own and owe today? The statement of profit or loss answers the first question. It covers a period of time, usually twelve months, so its heading says for the year ended 31 December 2026. The statement of financial position answers the second question. It is a snapshot of one day, so its heading says at 31 December 2026.

These statements matter. The owner uses them to judge performance and to decide how much can safely be taken as drawings. A bank uses them before lending, and the tax authorities use the profit to assess tax. The statement of financial position records the assets and liabilities on a specified date, so the owner can see the resources controlled and the debts to be paid.

The two statements are linked. The profit for the year flows from the statement of profit or loss into the capital section of the statement of financial position. If the profit figure is wrong, both statements are wrong. That is why examiners award marks for a correct method even when a figure carried forward is wrong, but you only get those marks if your working is clear.

Both statements are built from the trial balance plus the year-end adjustments. The rule to remember is simple: every balance in the trial balance appears once, and every adjustment appears twice, once in each statement.

Trading businesses and service businesses

A trading business buys goods and sells them at a higher price. A furniture shop, a pharmacy and a mobile phone retailer are trading businesses. Their statement of profit or loss has two sections. The trading section finds gross profit, which is revenue less cost of sales. The second section deducts expenses and adds other income to find profit for the year.

A service business sells skill or time instead of goods. A tutoring centre, a car repair garage, a hair salon and a delivery company are service businesses. They have no inventory of goods for resale and no cost of sales, so there is no gross profit. Their statement starts with fee income or revenue from services, adds any other income, and deducts expenses to reach profit for the year.

Some businesses do both. A garage that repairs cars and also sells tyres earns fee income and trading income, so its statement shows the trading section for the tyres and then adds the fee income below gross profit. A manufacturing sole trader first prepares a manufacturing account to find the cost of production (see 5.4), which replaces purchases in the cost of sales. In the exam, read the scenario carefully. If the business sells goods, you must calculate cost of sales. If it only provides a service, do not invent an inventory figure.

The trading section: finding gross profit

Gross profit is the profit from buying and selling goods. First find net revenue by deducting sales returns from revenue. Then find cost of sales. Start with opening inventory, add purchases, deduct purchases returns, add carriage inwards, and finally deduct closing inventory. The result is the cost of the goods that were actually sold during the year.

Why is carriage inwards added to purchases? Because it is the cost of getting the goods into the shop. Without paying it, the goods could not be sold, so it is part of their cost. Carriage outwards is different. It is the cost of delivering goods to customers after the sale, so it is a selling expense and belongs with the other expenses below gross profit.

Why is closing inventory deducted? Because those goods have not been sold yet. Charging them against this year's revenue would break the accruals (matching) concept. They will be charged next year, when they become next year's opening inventory. If you forget to deduct closing inventory, cost of sales is too high and gross profit is too low by the full amount.

From gross profit to profit for the year

Below gross profit, add any other income the business earned that did not come from selling goods. Common examples are discount received, rent received, commission received and a decrease in the allowance for irrecoverable debts. Then list every expense of the year and deduct the total.

Each expense must show the amount that belongs to this year, not the amount paid. This is where the Topic 4 adjustments are used. Add an accrued expense to the amount paid because the cost was used this year even though the bill is unpaid. Deduct a prepaid expense because part of the payment belongs to next year. Add depreciation for the year as an expense, whether it was found by the straight-line method, the reducing balance method, or the revaluation method (opening valuation plus purchases less closing valuation, used for small items such as loose tools). Add irrecoverable debts written off and any increase in the allowance for irrecoverable debts.

Profit for the year is gross profit plus other income less total expenses. If expenses are larger, the result is a loss for the year. A loss is not a sign that you made an arithmetic error, but it is a signal to check your work once more. Label the final line clearly. An unlabelled final figure, or one called by an older name such as net profit, risks losing the label mark.

The statement of financial position: layout and classification

The statement of financial position lists everything in a fixed order. Non-current assets come first, usually premises, then machinery or fixtures, then motor vehicles. Each is shown in three columns: cost, accumulated depreciation and carrying amount. Intangible assets, such as a patent, a trademark or a purchased software licence, have no physical form but benefit the business for years, so they are shown under their own heading, Intangible assets, within the non-current section. Then come current assets in order of how far they are from becoming cash: inventory, trade receivables less the allowance for irrecoverable debts, other receivables, bank and cash.

Current liabilities are deducted from current assets to give net current assets, also called working capital. Working capital shows whether the business can pay the debts due in the next year from assets that will become cash in the next year. Too little and suppliers may stop supplying; too much and money sits idle. Adding non-current assets gives a subtotal. Deducting non-current liabilities, such as a bank loan repayable in five years, gives net assets. The final section shows capital: opening capital, plus capital introduced, plus profit for the year, less drawings, equals closing capital. Net assets must equal closing capital. If they do not, something is missing or an adjustment has been entered once instead of twice.

Classification matters because users look at the groups, not only the total. A bank judges liquidity by comparing current assets with current liabilities. Putting a long-term loan among current liabilities would make the business look unable to pay its debts.

Drawings of goods, other assets and private expenses

Drawings are not only cash. The owner may take goods from the shelves for the family, take a business asset such as a laptop for private use, or let the business pay a private bill such as the owner's home electricity. All three are drawings, because value leaves the business for the owner's benefit. None of them is a business expense.

Goods taken are recorded at cost: debit drawings, credit purchases. Purchases fall, so cost of sales falls and gross profit is not distorted. A non-current asset taken is removed at its carrying amount: debit drawings with the carrying amount, debit provision for depreciation with the accumulated depreciation, and credit the asset account with its cost. A private expense paid by the business is taken out of the expense: debit drawings, credit the expense account, so the statement of profit or loss shows only the business part of the cost.

In the statement of financial position the total drawings, cash plus goods plus assets plus private expenses, is deducted from capital. If you leave goods taken in purchases, cost of sales is too high and profit is understated, and drawings are understated by the same amount, so closing capital is still correct but both the profit figure and the drawings figure are wrong.

5.2 · Study guide: Partnerships

A partnership is a business owned by two or more people who share the profits according to an agreement. After the profit for the year is found in the usual way, an appropriation account divides it between the partners through interest on capital, salaries, interest on drawings and the profit-sharing ratio. Each partner's share is then recorded in a current account, and the statement of financial position shows capital and current accounts for every partner.

Why form a partnership?

A sole trader who wants to grow often reaches a limit. One person can only provide so much capital, so many hours and so many skills. Taking a partner solves these problems. Two or more owners can invest more capital, share the workload and the worry, cover for each other during illness or holidays, and bring different skills, for example one partner who is good at selling and one who is good at managing money. Losses are shared as well as profits.

There are costs too. Profits must be shared, so each owner may receive less than before. Decisions take longer because partners must agree, and disagreements can damage the business. Each partner is normally liable for the whole of the partnership's debts, not just their own share, and one partner's mistake binds the others. A partnership also ends in law when a partner leaves or dies, so a new agreement is needed.

The exam often asks for two advantages and two disadvantages. Give reasons, not just headings: more capital so the business can expand, shared decision making so decisions are slower.

The partnership agreement

Because partners share profit, they need to agree in advance how to share it. The partnership agreement is important because it sets the rules before any problem arises, so disputes are avoided or can be settled by pointing to what was agreed. It is usually written, and it normally states how much capital each partner contributes, the profit-sharing ratio, whether partners receive interest on capital and at what rate, whether any partner receives a salary, whether interest is charged on drawings and at what rate, the rate of interest on any loan from a partner, and how much each partner may draw.

Each item has a purpose. Interest on capital rewards the partner who invested more money, so the partner with $60,000 invested receives more than the partner with $40,000 before the remaining profit is shared. A salary rewards the partner who works longer hours or carries more responsibility. Interest on drawings discourages partners from taking cash early in the year, because the business needs that cash to operate. The profit-sharing ratio then divides whatever is left.

Where there is no agreement, the law of the country applies. In countries that follow the Partnership Act 1890, profits and losses are shared equally, no salaries or interest on capital are given, no interest is charged on drawings, and a partner who lends money to the firm receives interest at 5% per year on the loan.

Expense or appropriation? The key distinction

The most important idea in this section is the difference between an expense and an appropriation. An expense is a cost of running the business and is deducted in the statement of profit or loss to find profit for the year. An appropriation is a division of that profit between the owners and appears only in the appropriation account.

Interest on a loan from a partner is an expense. The partner is acting as a lender, like a bank, so the interest is a cost of borrowing and is charged before profit for the year is found. If the interest has not been paid by the year end, it is accrued as a current liability. A partner's salary is an appropriation, even though it is called a salary. The partner is an owner, not an employee, so the salary is a share of profit. Interest on capital is also an appropriation.

The consequence of getting this wrong is serious. Putting a partner's salary of $9,000 in expenses understates profit for the year by $9,000 and then shares the wrong residual. The partner still receives the salary, but every other figure is wrong.

Capital accounts and current accounts

Most partnerships keep two accounts for each partner. The capital account is fixed. Loans from partners

A partner may lend money to the business in addition to capital. The loan is not capital. It is a liability of the partnership, shown as a non-current liability if it is repayable after more than one year, or as a current liability if it is due within the year. The partner is entitled to interest on the loan at the agreed rate, and that interest is an expense in the statement of profit or loss, exactly like interest paid to a bank.

If the interest has not been paid by the year end, add the unpaid amount as an expense and show it as other payables. Some agreements allow unpaid loan interest to be credited to the partner's current account instead; if the question says so, follow it.

Why does this matter in practice? A partner with a loan ranks with other lenders and has a stronger claim to repayment than a partner's capital. In the exam, read whether the profit figure given is before or after loan interest. If it is before, deduct the interest first to find profit for the year, then begin the appropriation account.

The statement of financial position of a partnership

The asset and liability sections of a partnership's statement of financial position are the same as for a sole trader. Non-current assets, current assets, current liabilities, net current assets and non-current liabilities are presented in the same way, and a loan from a partner appears with the liabilities.

The difference is the final section. Instead of one capital figure there are capital accounts and current accounts for each partner, usually shown in columns with a total. A debit balance on a current account is deducted. The total of capital accounts plus current accounts must equal net assets.

5.3 · Study guide: Limited companies

A limited company is a separate legal person owned by shareholders whose liability is limited to the amount they agreed to pay for their shares. Its finance comes from ordinary shares, preference shares, debentures and retained profits. Its financial statements add a statement of changes in equity, and its statement of financial position shows equity made up of share capital and reserves instead of an owner's capital.

What makes a company different

A limited company is a separate legal person. It can own assets, owe debts, sign contracts and be taken to court in its own name. The owners are the shareholders, and the people who run it day to day are the directors. In a small company the same people may be both, but in the accounts they are kept apart. Directors' salaries are expenses of the company; dividends to shareholders are a distribution of profit. Like a sole trader or partnership, a company can be a trading business, a service business, a manufacturing business or a combination, and the same 5.1 adjustments apply to its statements.

The great advantage is limited liability. If the company fails, a shareholder loses the money paid for the shares and nothing more. A sole trader or partner can lose personal possessions. Because of this protection, companies can raise large amounts of capital from many investors, and the company continues when a shareholder dies or sells shares.

The disadvantages are cost and openness. Setting up a company involves legal formalities, the accounts must follow detailed rules and be filed where the public can read them, and the original owners may lose control if they sell too many shares. In the exam, when asked about advantages and disadvantages, link each point to a reason.

Sources of finance: shares and debentures

A company raises long-term finance in three main ways. Ordinary shares are the risk capital. Ordinary shareholders vote at meetings, own the reserves and receive whatever dividend the directors recommend after everyone else has been paid. In a bad year they may receive nothing. Preference shares carry a fixed rate of dividend, for example 6% of the nominal value, which is paid before any ordinary dividend. Preference shareholders usually have no vote, and if there is no profit the dividend is normally not paid.

Debentures are not shares at all. A debenture is a long-term loan, often secured on the company's assets, with a fixed rate of interest and a repayment date. Debenture holders are lenders, not owners. Their interest is an expense of the company and must be paid whether or not there is a profit. If it is not paid, the debenture holders can take action against the company.

The difference matters when reading a statement of financial position. Shares are part of equity. Debentures are a non-current liability. A company that borrows heavily through debentures has fixed interest to pay every year, which increases risk when profits fall.

Issued, called-up and paid-up capital

Shares have a nominal value, such as $1 or $0.50, which is the amount shown as share capital. The issued share capital is the total nominal value of the shares the company has sold to shareholders. A company does not always ask for the whole price at once. The called-up share capital is the amount the company has demanded so far. The paid-up share capital is the amount shareholders have actually paid in response.

Suppose Horizon Toys Ltd issues 80,000 ordinary shares of $1 each and calls $0.75 per share. One shareholder holding 4,000 shares has not paid the latest call of $0.25. Issued capital is $80,000. Called-up capital is 80,000 × $0.75 = $60,000. Paid-up capital is $60,000 − (4,000 × $0.25) = $59,000. The unpaid $1,000 is calls in arrears.

Dividends are usually expressed per share or as a percentage of nominal value. A dividend of $0.05 per share on 80,000 shares costs $4,000. A 6% dividend on 40,000 preference shares of $1 costs $2,400. Always read whether the nominal value is $1 or something else before multiplying.

Reserves and retained earnings

Reserves are profits that belong to the shareholders but have not been paid out as dividends. They are part of equity. Retained earnings is the running total of profits kept in the business since the company began. Each year it increases by the profit for the year and decreases by dividends paid and by any transfer to another reserve. A general reserve is created when the directors decide to move part of retained earnings into a separate reserve. This signals that they intend to keep that profit in the business, for example to replace assets or to cushion against future losses.

A transfer to the general reserve does not change total equity. It moves $10,000 from one column to another. Nor does it change cash. This is the point students most often misunderstand. Reserves are a claim of the shareholders on the company's net assets, not a pile of money. A company with a general reserve of $30,000 may have only $2,000 in the bank, because the retained profits have been used to buy inventory, machinery or to pay off loans.

Dividends can only be paid out of profits available for distribution, in practice retained earnings. A large reserve shows that the company has been profitable in the past, but the ability to pay a dividend in cash depends on the bank balance.

The statement of profit or loss of a company

The statement of profit or loss of a company follows the same structure as a sole trader's, with a few differences in presentation. Revenue less cost of sales gives gross profit. Other income is added. Expenses are often grouped into distribution costs and administrative expenses, and directors' remuneration is included as an expense because directors are employees of the company. The result is sometimes labelled profit from operations.

Debenture interest is then deducted as a finance cost. It is an expense because debenture holders are lenders. If the full year's interest has not been paid by the year end, the unpaid part is accrued, charged in full and shown as other payables. For example, 8% debentures of $50,000 give interest of $4,000. If only $2,000 was paid, the expense is still $4,000 and $2,000 is a current liability.

The final line is profit for the year. Dividends are not deducted in the statement of profit or loss. They are a distribution of profit to the owners and appear in the statement of changes in equity. The 0452 syllabus does not require the calculation of taxation.

The asset side of a company's statement of financial position is the same as for any business: non-current assets at cost, accumulated depreciation and carrying amount, then current assets. 5.4 · Study guide: Manufacturing accounts

A manufacturer makes the goods it sells, so before it can calculate cost of sales it must calculate the cost of producing finished goods. The manufacturing account collects direct materials, direct labour and direct expenses into prime cost, adds factory overheads, adjusts for work in progress and arrives at the cost of production. That figure is transferred to the statement of profit or loss in place of purchases.

Why a manufacturer needs an extra account

A trader buys finished goods and sells them. Its cost of sales starts with purchases. A manufacturer does not buy finished goods. It buys raw materials, pays workers to turn them into products, and pays for a factory in which this happens. Before it can find the cost of the goods it sold, it must first find the cost of the goods it made. This is the job of the manufacturing account.

The manufacturing account is prepared before the statement of profit or loss and covers the same year. It has three stages. First it collects the direct costs into prime cost. Then it adds the indirect factory costs, called factory overheads. Finally it adjusts for work in progress to find the cost of production, meaning the cost of the goods actually completed in the year. That figure is carried to the trading section of the statement of profit or loss, where it takes the place that purchases would occupy for a trader.

Everything that happens in the factory belongs in the manufacturing account. Everything that happens in the office, the warehouse for finished goods or the sales department belongs in the statement of profit or loss. The first skill in this section is sorting costs into the right place.

Direct costs and prime cost

A direct cost can be traced to the product. If a workshop makes wooden tables, the timber in each table is a direct material and the wages of the carpenter who builds it are direct labour. A royalty paid to a designer for every table made is a direct expense. Add the three together and you have prime cost.

Raw materials need a small calculation of their own, because the business does not use exactly what it buys. Start with the opening inventory of raw materials, add purchases of raw materials, deduct any returns, add carriage inwards on those purchases, and deduct the closing inventory of raw materials. The result is the cost of raw materials consumed, which is the amount actually used in production this year.

Direct wages are adjusted like any expense. If wages of $52,600 were paid and $1,400 was still owing at the year end, the direct labour for the year is $54,000. Why does the distinction between direct and indirect matter? Because prime cost tells the owner the cost that rises and falls with every extra unit made. Overheads mostly stay the same whether the factory makes 1,000 tables or 1,200.

Factory overheads and apportionment

Factory overheads are the indirect costs of running the factory. Typical examples are indirect wages such as supervisors, cleaners and maintenance staff, factory rent and rates, factory power and lighting, factory insurance, depreciation of plant and machinery, and indirect materials such as lubricants and cleaning supplies. They are listed and totalled, then added to prime cost.

Some expenses are shared between the factory and the office. Rent, insurance and electricity are common examples. The question will tell you how to split them, for example rent to be apportioned three quarters to the factory and one quarter to the office, or in the ratio 2:1. Calculate each share carefully. The factory share goes into factory overheads; the office share goes into the expenses of the statement of profit or loss.

Accruals and prepayments apply here too. If factory rent of $12,000 was paid and $1,000 of it relates to next year, the overhead for this year is $11,000 and the $1,000 is a current asset. Depreciation of factory machinery is always a factory overhead, while depreciation of office equipment or delivery vehicles is an expense below gross profit. Getting this wrong moves cost between the manufacturing account and the statement of profit or loss, so cost of production and gross profit are both misstated, even though profit for the year may be unchanged.

Work in progress and the cost of production

At the end of the year some products are half made. They have used materials and labour, but they are not finished goods and cannot be sold. This partly finished inventory is called work in progress. The cost spent on it this year must not be charged to this year's completed output, so closing work in progress is deducted. In the same way, the opening work in progress was paid for last year but completed this year, so it is added.

The formula is: prime cost plus factory overheads equals total factory cost; add opening work in progress; deduct closing work in progress; the result is the cost of production, also called the factory cost of production or the production cost of goods completed. This is the figure transferred to the statement of profit or loss.

A manufacturer holds three kinds of inventory at the year end, and all three are current assets. Raw materials are the unused materials in the store. Work in progress is the partly finished output on the factory floor. Finished goods are the completed products in the warehouse waiting to be sold. Each is listed separately under current assets, usually with a subtotal for inventories.

All three closing inventories are the same figures used in the manufacturing account and the statement of profit or loss. For Hatem at 31 December 2026, inventories are raw materials $8,700, work in progress $7,100 and finished goods $17,900, total $33,700. The same figures become the opening inventories next year.

The rest of the statement of financial position is prepared in the usual way. Factory machinery and office equipment are both non-current assets shown at cost less accumulated depreciation; the split between factory and office matters only for deciding where the depreciation charge goes. Accrued direct wages and prepaid factory rent are other payables and other receivables like any other accrual or prepayment.

Reading the manufacturing account

The manufacturing account is not only a step towards profit. It gives the owner useful information. Dividing the cost of production by the number of units completed gives the cost per unit. If a workshop produced 30,500 candles at a total cost of $91,500, each candle cost $3.00 to make. The owner can compare this with the price a supplier would charge for ready-made candles and decide whether to continue manufacturing or buy in.

That decision should not rest on the cost figure alone. If the factory closed, some overheads such as rent on a lease and depreciation might continue, so the saving would be less than the full cost per unit. The quality and reliability of the supplier, the effect on workers who would lose their jobs and the risk of becoming dependent on one supplier all matter. Exam questions in this section often end with a short evaluation like this, and the best answers use the numbers and then add two or three non-financial points.

Also watch the split between prime cost and overheads. If overheads are a large share of total cost, the business has high fixed costs and its profit will fall quickly when output falls.

5.5 · Study guide: Clubs and societies

Clubs and societies exist to provide activities for their members, not to make a profit. Their treasurer keeps a receipts and payments account, which is a summary of the cash book, but the members need an income and expenditure account, which applies the accruals (matching) concept to find the surplus or deficit for the year, and a statement of financial position in which the accumulated fund takes the place of capital.

Clubs are not businesses, but they still need accounts

A sports club, a chess society or a gardening association is run for its members. It has no owner, it does not aim to make a profit, and the people who run it are usually volunteers elected at the annual general meeting. The treasurer looks after the money. Because members pay subscriptions, they want to know how their money has been used, whether the club can pay its bills, and whether subscriptions should go up or down.

So clubs use the same accounting ideas as businesses, with different names. A business measures profit; a club measures a surplus or a deficit. A business has capital; a club has an accumulated fund. A business prepares a statement of profit or loss; a club prepares an income and expenditure account. The statement of financial position keeps its name.

The reason for the different names is the different purpose. A surplus is not profit to be paid to anyone. It stays in the club to improve facilities or keep subscriptions down. In the exam, use the club words. Writing profit instead of surplus, or capital instead of accumulated fund, can cost marks.

Receipts and payments account: what it shows and what it misses

Many treasurers keep only a cash book. At the year end they summarise it as a receipts and payments account. On the debit side are the opening cash and bank balances and every receipt of the year: subscriptions, cafe sales, donations, ticket money, a loan received. On the credit side are every payment: rent, wages, purchases for the cafe, new equipment, prizes. The balancing figure is the closing cash and bank balance.

This account is easy to prepare and easy to understand, but it has serious limits. It records cash, not income and expenditure. Subscriptions received include money for next year and leave out money still owed. Rent paid may cover only ten months. The cost of new equipment appears in full, even though it will be used for years. Depreciation does not appear at all because no cash is paid. So the closing balance tells members how much money the club has, but not whether the club lived within its means this year.

That is why the income and expenditure account is needed. It takes the receipts and payments account as a starting point and adjusts every figure to the amount that belongs to the year.

The subscriptions account

Subscriptions are usually the largest source of income, and the exam almost always tests them. The subscriptions account is a ledger account that converts cash received into income earned. It uses the same logic as any account with accruals and prepayments at both ends of the year.

On the debit side: opening subscriptions in arrears (an asset brought down), the income for the year transferred to the income and expenditure account, and closing subscriptions in advance (a liability carried down). On the credit side: opening subscriptions in advance (a liability brought down), cash received during the year, any subscriptions written off as irrecoverable, and closing subscriptions in arrears (an asset carried down). The income figure is the balancing amount.

As a formula: income = cash received + closing arrears − opening arrears + opening advance − closing advance + subscriptions written off. Writing off subscriptions reduces the asset, and the write-off is shown as an expense in the income and expenditure account. The income and expenditure account

The income and expenditure account is prepared like a statement of profit or loss. On the income side list subscriptions for the year from the subscriptions account, the profit on each trading activity, donations, and any other income earned such as entry fees or interest. On the expenditure side list each expense adjusted to the amount for the year: rent plus owing less prepaid, insurance less prepaid, and so on. Include depreciation of the club's equipment. Include subscriptions written off. Do not include the purchase of equipment, because it is capital expenditure that becomes a non-current asset. Do not include loan repayments or loans received.

If income is larger, the difference is the surplus of income over expenditure. If expenditure is larger, it is the deficit. The surplus is added to the accumulated fund in the statement of financial position.

The account can be set out as a ledger account with two sides or in vertical form like a statement of profit or loss. Either layout is accepted, but the vertical form is easier to check. Head it income and expenditure account for the year ended with the date.

A club has no owner who introduced capital, so where does the accumulated fund come from? It is simply the total of everything the club owns less everything it owes. 5.6 · Study guide: Incomplete records

Many small traders do not keep full double entry records, yet they still need to know their profit and their financial position. The accountant rebuilds the missing figures: a statement of affairs gives capital, comparing opening and closing capital gives profit, control accounts give credit sales and purchases, and margin or mark-up gives gross profit, cost of sales or missing inventory.

Why records are incomplete and why it matters

Many small traders keep only what they need day to day: a cash book or bank account, a file of invoices, and a notebook of who owes them money. They do not keep a full double entry ledger because it takes time, skill and money, and for a small business with few transactions they may think it is unnecessary. Records can also be incomplete because of fire, flood, theft or a computer failure. In the 0452 exam, incomplete records questions are set only for sole traders running trading businesses.

Not keeping full records has real advantages: it is cheaper, since no trained book-keeper or accounting software is needed; it takes less of the owner's time; and the records are simple enough for the owner to understand. The disadvantages are serious. Without full records the trader cannot prepare a trial balance to find errors, cannot easily prove profit to the tax authority or show a bank reliable figures for a loan, cannot see at a glance what each customer owes, and finds fraud or theft harder to detect. Decisions about prices, credit and expansion are made by guesswork, and an accountant must be paid at the year end to rebuild the figures.

That accountant reconstructs the missing figures from the evidence available. This section teaches the tools: the statement of affairs, the capital comparison, control accounts, cash summaries, and the ratios mark-up, margin and rate of inventory turnover. Most exam questions combine two or more of them.

The statement of affairs

A statement of affairs lists the assets and liabilities of a business at one date. Then every liability: trade payables, accrued expenses, loans, bank overdraft. Take care with the date. The opening statement uses opening values only. If the question gives depreciation for the year, the closing statement must use the lower carrying amount.

Profit from the change in capital

Capital changes for only four reasons: profit increases it, a loss decreases it, drawings decrease it and capital introduced increases it. So if you know opening capital, closing capital, drawings and capital introduced, you can find profit. Profit = closing capital − opening capital + drawings − capital introduced. If the answer is negative, the business made a loss.

Why add drawings? Because the owner took money out that profit had put in. If profit was $11,200 and the owner drew $11,300, closing capital is lower than opening capital even though the business was profitable. Adding the drawings back reveals the true profit. Why deduct capital introduced? Because money the owner paid in did not come from trading.

This method gives the profit for the year but no detail. It cannot tell the owner the gross profit, the expenses or where money was lost. For that the accountant must rebuild the statement of profit or loss using the next two tools. In the exam, the capital comparison is often the final check: the profit from the reconstructed statement of profit or loss should match the profit from the capital comparison.

Margin and mark-up

Margin and mark-up both describe gross profit, but from different directions. Margin is gross profit as a percentage of revenue. Mark-up is gross profit as a percentage of cost of sales. If goods costing $80 are sold for $100, gross profit is $20, the mark-up is 25% of cost and the margin is 20% of selling price. The same profit gives a smaller margin than mark-up, because the selling price is larger than the cost.

To convert, think in fractions. A mark-up of 1/4 means cost is 4 parts and selling price is 5 parts, so the margin is 1/5. A margin of 1/4 means selling price is 4 parts and cost is 3 parts, so the mark-up is 1/3. The table shows the common pairs.

These ratios unlock missing figures. If revenue is $60,000 and the margin is 20%, gross profit is $12,000 and cost of sales is $48,000. If cost of sales is $48,000 and the mark-up is 25%, gross profit is $12,000 and revenue is $60,000. Once cost of sales is known, the inventory equation gives closing inventory: opening inventory + purchases − cost of sales. This is how the value of inventory destroyed in a fire or stolen in a burglary is estimated for an insurance claim.

Using inventory turnover to find missing figures

The rate of inventory turnover tells you how many times in a year the average inventory is sold and replaced. Rate of inventory turnover (times) = cost of sales ÷ average inventory, where average inventory = (opening inventory + closing inventory) ÷ 2. The same idea in days is inventory turnover (days) = average inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365.

Because the formula links three figures, any one can be found from the other two. Cost of sales = rate of inventory turnover × average inventory. Average inventory = cost of sales ÷ rate of inventory turnover. Once average inventory is known, closing inventory = (2 × average inventory) − opening inventory. Then purchases can be found from the inventory equation: purchases = cost of sales + closing inventory − opening inventory.

Example: a trader's revenue was $63,000, goods are sold at a mark-up of 25%, opening inventory was $7,600 and the rate of inventory turnover is 6 times. Cost of sales = $63,000 × 100/125 = $50,400. Average inventory = $50,400 ÷ 6 = $8,400. Closing inventory = $16,800 − $7,600 = $9,200. Purchases = $50,400 + $9,200 − $7,600 = $52,000. Always say which formula you are rearranging, so the examiner can follow your method.

Using a cash summary to find a missing figure

Cash is where records are weakest, because coins and notes leave no bank statement. A cash summary rebuilds the cash account. Debit the opening cash balance and all cash received, mainly cash sales. Credit all cash paid out: amounts banked, cash expenses, wages paid in cash, cash purchases. Carry down the closing cash balance. Whatever is missing is the balancing figure. Usually it is drawings, because owners often take cash from the till without writing it down. Sometimes the question says cash was stolen, and the balancing figure is the amount lost.

In the fourth worked example, opening cash was $400, cash sales $38,500, cash banked $29,600, cash expenses $3,900, wages $2,800 and closing cash $350. The missing amount is $2,250, which is drawings.

The same approach works for the bank. If the question gives all bank receipts and payments except one, rebuild the bank account and find the gap. Always ask which figure the question has left out; that figure is the balancing item. Then carry it to the right place: drawings to the capital section, cash purchases to purchases, stolen cash to expenses.

Putting it together: full statements from incomplete records

A full incomplete records question gives a list of opening balances, a summary of the bank account, a list of closing balances and a few notes. The order of work is always the same. First, prepare an opening statement of affairs to find opening capital. Second, use total receivables and payables accounts to find credit sales and purchases, and add any cash sales and cash purchases. Third, use a cash summary if a cash figure is missing. Fourth, prepare the statement of profit or loss using these sales and purchases, the inventories given, and expenses adjusted for accruals and prepayments and depreciation. Fifth, prepare the statement of financial position, taking the closing figures from the question.

Finally, check. Opening capital plus profit less drawings plus capital introduced should equal the closing capital shown by net assets. If it does, every reconstructed figure fits together. If it does not, the most common causes are a missing cash drawing, discount allowed or received left out of the control account, or an accrual used in the statement of profit or loss but omitted from the statement of financial position.

It is a long question, but every step uses a skill from earlier topics. Keep the workings labelled and in order, and the marks follow.

6. Analysis and interpretation

6.1 · Profitability ratios

Gross profit margin = gross profit/revenue ×100; mark-up = gross profit/cost of sales ×100.

Profit margin is profit for the year divided by revenue times 100. ROCE is profit for the year before interest divided by capital employed times 100. For a limited company, capital employed = issued shares + reserves + non-current liabilities; for a sole trader or partnership, capital employed = capital (partners' capital and current accounts) + non-current liabilities. The syllabus formulas are the only ones accepted, so do not mix in alternative textbook definitions. Profitability measures return and cost control, not immediate cash availability.

Revenue $100,000; cost of sales $60,000; profit $10,000. Gross profit margin 40%, mark-up 66.67%, profit margin 10%. Profit for the year $10,000 after loan interest $2,000 gives profit before interest $12,000; with capital employed $80,000, ROCE = 15%.

6.1 · Liquidity ratios

Current (working capital) ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities; acid test (liquid) ratio = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities.

Present both liquidity ratios as a ratio, x : 1. The acid test removes inventory because inventory is often slower to turn into cash. A high ratio may reflect idle funds or slow receivables, not efficient management. A low ratio may indicate pressure, but payment patterns and industry context matter. Use total current liabilities, and avoid treating all non-current liabilities as immediate obligations.

6.1 · Efficiency ratios

Rate of inventory turnover (times) = cost of sales ÷ average inventory.

Average inventory = (opening inventory + closing inventory) ÷ 2. Inventory turnover (days) = average inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365. Trade receivables turnover (days) = trade receivables ÷ credit sales × 365; trade payables turnover (days) = trade payables ÷ credit purchases × 365. Use the credit totals specified, not total revenue or cash purchases. Report days and times distinctly. 6.2 · Working-capital decisions

Working capital = current assets − current liabilities.

Slow-moving inventory ties up funds; reduce unnecessary inventory while avoiding shortages. Offer suitable credit terms and follow up receivables without driving away reliable customers. Compare a recommendation with the specific ratios, cash pattern and non-financial context.

Make a supported judgement with comparable periods and policies; do not rank businesses by one ratio alone.

Financial figures are useful evidence, not a complete picture.

Statements do not fully measure workforce skill, management quality, location, customer loyalty or economic conditions. Published information also describes a past period. 6.1 · Study guide: Calculation and understanding of accounting ratios

A ratio puts two figures from the financial statements together so that the result can be compared. This section gives the ten ratios in the 0452 syllabus, the only formulas Cambridge accepts, the unit for each answer, and the exact figures to pick from a statement of profit or loss and a statement of financial position.

Why we calculate ratios at all

A profit of $36,000 sounds good. But is it good? It depends on the sales that produced it and on the money invested to earn it. A profit of $36,000 on revenue of $240,000 is very different from the same profit on revenue of $2,400,000. A ratio removes the problem of size by relating one figure to another. The result can then be compared with last year, with another business, or with a target.

In 0452 there are ten ratios, and the syllabus says that only its formulas are accepted. Four measure profitability: gross profit margin, mark-up, profit margin and return on capital employed (ROCE). Two measure liquidity: the current (working capital) ratio and the acid test (liquid) ratio. Four measure how efficiently working capital is managed: rate of inventory turnover (times), inventory turnover (days), trade receivables turnover (days) and trade payables turnover (days). Learn each formula and each name exactly. A correct formula with the right figures can earn method marks even if you slip in the arithmetic, so always show the working.

The ten ratios: formula, unit and what a change means

The table below is the heart of this section. Each row gives the syllabus formula, the unit in which the answer must be written, and the first thing to think when the figure moves. Always write the unit. A gross profit margin of 40 is incomplete; 40% is right. A current (working capital) ratio of 2.2 is incomplete; 2.20:1 is complete, because the syllabus says the two liquidity ratios are presented as a ratio. Rate of inventory turnover is in times; the other three efficiency ratios are in days. Examiners cannot give a mark for a number whose unit is missing, because they cannot tell what you have calculated.

Profitability: where each figure comes from

Gross profit margin and profit margin both divide by revenue. Mark-up divides the same gross profit by cost of sales instead. Revenue is sales less sales returns. Gross profit is revenue less cost of sales. Profit for the year is gross profit plus other income less all expenses. Take these figures straight from the statement of profit or loss. Because cost of sales is smaller than revenue, the mark-up is always larger than the gross profit margin: a business that buys goods for $100 and sells them for $125 has a mark-up of 25% and a gross profit margin of 20%.

ROCE is different. The top of the fraction is profit for the year before interest, because capital employed includes long-term loans and the interest is the reward paid to those lenders. So add loan or debenture interest back to profit for the year. For a limited company, capital employed is issued shares plus reserves plus non-current liabilities. For a sole trader it is capital plus non-current liabilities. Use the figures in the statement of financial position; this is the only formula Cambridge accepts.

Liquidity: current (working capital) ratio and acid test (liquid) ratio

Liquidity means the ability to pay debts when they fall due. The current (working capital) ratio compares all current assets with all current liabilities. Current assets are inventory, trade receivables, other receivables such as prepayments, bank and cash. Current liabilities are trade payables, other payables such as accruals, and any bank overdraft. A bank overdraft is always a current liability, even if the business has had it for years, because the bank can demand repayment at any time.

The acid test (liquid) ratio removes inventory from the current assets. Inventory is the least liquid current asset: it must first be sold, often on credit, and then the customer must pay. If a supplier demands payment next week, inventory cannot be used. So the acid test (liquid) ratio asks a harder question: could the business pay its short-term debts tomorrow? The syllabus says both answers are presented as a ratio, so write them as x:1 with two decimal places, for example 1.32:1, not as a percentage.

Efficiency: the four turnover ratios

Rate of inventory turnover (times) is cost of sales divided by average inventory. Use cost of sales, not revenue, because inventory is valued at cost; comparing cost with cost gives a true number of times. Average inventory is opening inventory plus closing inventory divided by two. Inventory turnover (days) turns the same two figures upside down: average inventory divided by cost of sales, multiplied by 365. A business that turns its inventory over 7.30 times holds goods for 50 days on average. Use the syllabus formula for the days; dividing 365 by the times is only a check.

Trade receivables turnover in days is trade receivables divided by credit sales, multiplied by 365. Cash sales create no receivables, so they must be left out. If a question says that 60% of sales were on credit, multiply revenue by 60% before you divide. Trade payables turnover in days is trade payables divided by credit purchases, multiplied by 365. Again, cash purchases are excluded. If the question says all purchases were on credit, total purchases are the credit purchases. Work out the days to two decimal places. Mark schemes normally accept either the decimal answer or the answer rounded up to the next whole day, because part of a day still counts: 39.54 days or 40 days.

A routine for the exam

Follow the same four steps every time. First, write the name of the ratio and its formula in words. Second, write the figures you are using, with their source: gross profit $94,000 and revenue $240,000. Third, show the division and multiplication. Fourth, write the answer with its unit and two decimal places, or whole days. This routine takes a few seconds and protects you. If you pick a wrong figure, the examiner can still award the formula mark. If your calculator slips, the examiner can still see the correct method.

Before you calculate, read the statements and tick the figures you will need: revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, profit for the year, interest, opening and closing inventory, trade receivables, trade payables, current assets, current liabilities, capital and non-current liabilities. Doing this first stops you using a total where a part is needed, such as total sales instead of credit sales.

Comparison statements and a good interpretation

A comparison statement sets out the results of two years side by side so that changes can be seen at a glance. Profitability ratios: meaning and causes of change

The gross profit margin and the profit margin are the two main indicators of a business's profitability: the first shows how well it buys and prices its goods, the second shows how well it also controls its expenses. Gross profit margin depends only on selling prices and the cost of the goods sold. It is not affected by rent, wages or any other expense. So if gross profit margin falls, look at prices, purchase costs, the mix of goods sold, wastage, theft, or a mistake in valuing closing inventory. If gross profit margin is steady but profit margin falls, the problem is below gross profit: expenses have risen faster than revenue, or other income has fallen. Mark-up always moves in the same direction as gross profit margin, because both measure the same gross profit, so the same causes explain a change in either.

Return on capital employed links profit to the money invested. It falls when profit falls, or when more capital is put in without earning more. A new loan spent on equipment that is not yet productive lowers ROCE this year. Compare ROCE with what the owner could earn elsewhere. If a bank deposit pays 4% and the business earns 20%, the capital is working well. If ROCE is below the rate charged on the loan, borrowing is costing more than it earns.

What changes gross profit and profit for the year

Gross profit is revenue less cost of sales, so anything that changes either figure changes gross profit. The syllabus names four causes. Selling prices: a price rise with the same quantity sold increases revenue but not cost of sales, so gross profit and the gross profit margin both rise; a price cut does the opposite. Purchasing prices: if suppliers charge more and selling prices stay the same, cost of sales rises and gross profit and the margin fall. Sales quantity: selling more units at the same prices and costs increases gross profit in dollars, but the gross profit margin does not change, because revenue and cost of sales rise in proportion. Valuation of inventory: closing inventory is deducted in cost of sales, so if it is overvalued by $2,000, cost of sales is $2,000 too low and gross profit is $2,000 too high.

Profit for the year is gross profit plus other income less expenses. It therefore changes when gross profit changes, when other income such as rent received or discount received rises or falls, and when expenses such as wages, rent, depreciation or irrecoverable debts change. If the gross profit margin is steady but the profit margin falls, look below gross profit.

Liquidity ratios: meaning and the danger of too much or too little

A current (working capital) ratio of 2.20:1 means the business has $2.20 of current assets for every $1 of debts due within a year. A figure between about 1.5:1 and 2:1 is often described as comfortable, but this is a guide, not a rule. A supermarket that sells for cash and holds fast-moving inventory can be safe at 0.9:1. A furniture maker that gives three months of credit needs more.

A ratio that is too high is also a problem. Cash sitting in the bank earns little. Inventory sitting in the storeroom costs money to hold and may go out of date. Receivables that are not collected may never be paid. The acid test (liquid) ratio shows whether the business could pay its debts without selling inventory. A acid test (liquid) ratio well below 1:1 together with a healthy current (working capital) ratio tells you that the current assets are mostly inventory, and that is a warning sign: the business is holding too much inventory, or inventory that is not selling.

Efficiency ratios: inventory, receivables and payables

A falling rate of inventory turnover (times), which is the same message as a rising inventory turnover (days), means goods stay longer on the shelf. Causes include buying too much, a fall in demand, poor product choice, or holding slow lines. The consequence is cash tied up and a higher risk of obsolete goods. Rising trade receivables turnover (days) means customers pay more slowly. Causes include weaker credit control, longer credit terms offered to win sales, or a large new customer with slow habits. The consequence is later cash and more irrecoverable debts.

Trade payables turnover (days) works the other way. Taking longer to pay keeps cash in the business, which helps liquidity. But a rising figure may mean the business cannot pay on time. Suppliers may withdraw credit, refuse to supply, or stop giving cash discounts. The healthy pattern is to collect from customers faster than you pay suppliers. If trade receivables turnover is 50 days and trade payables turnover is 30 days, the business is lending to its customers for 20 days longer than its suppliers lend to it, and the bank overdraft fills the gap.

Actions to improve profitability, with their drawbacks

There are only two ways to raise profit: more revenue for the same cost, or less cost for the same revenue. To raise gross profit margin, increase selling prices, find cheaper suppliers, buy in bulk for trade discount, change the mix of goods towards higher-margin lines, or reduce wastage and theft. To raise profit margin, control expenses: review rent, staffing, advertising and energy. To raise ROCE, earn more profit from the same capital, or sell assets that are not being used.

Every action has a cost. Higher prices may lose customers to competitors. Cheaper suppliers may mean lower quality and more returns. Cutting advertising may cut future sales. Cutting wages may lose skilled staff. Selling an asset brings cash once but may reduce capacity. A good answer names the action and then says but, followed by the drawback. That is what the mark scheme rewards.

Actions to improve liquidity and working capital, with their drawbacks

Liquidity improves when cash comes in sooner, goes out later, or when long-term money replaces short-term debt. Collect receivables faster by sending statements promptly, chasing overdue accounts, offering a small cash discount, and checking new customers before giving credit. Reduce inventory to the level needed, and sell off slow lines, even at a reduced price. Negotiate longer credit from suppliers. Replace a bank overdraft with a long-term loan, or introduce more capital. Delay buying non-current assets, or lease instead of buying. Reduce drawings.

Again, each step has a drawback. Cash discounts reduce profit. Pressing customers may drive them to competitors. Low inventory may cause lost sales when a customer wants goods now. Paying suppliers late may lose discounts and goodwill. A long-term loan brings interest and must be repaid. Lower drawings affect the owner's own life. Notice that paying a supplier with cash does not improve a current (working capital) ratio that is already below 1:1; it makes it worse, because when current assets are smaller than current liabilities, taking the same amount off both makes the ratio fall further.

Profit is not cash

Students often ask how a business with a profit of $36,000 can have a bank overdraft. Profit is measured by matching revenue earned with expenses incurred, whether or not the cash has moved. Cash is what is actually in the bank. The two differ for several reasons. Credit sales count as revenue before the customer pays. Purchases of inventory use cash before the goods are sold. Buying a van uses cash at once but is charged to profit slowly through depreciation. Drawings take cash but are not an expense. Loan repayments take cash but are not an expense; only the interest is. In the other direction, a new loan or extra capital brings cash in but is not income, so it is not profit.

This is why we need both sets of ratios. Profitability ratios tell us whether the business model works. Liquidity ratios tell us whether the business will survive long enough to enjoy the profit. A fast-growing, profitable business that gives generous credit and fills its storeroom can run out of cash. That situation is called overtrading, and the ratios show it: rising revenue, rising trade receivables turnover (days), falling inventory turnover, falling acid test (liquid) ratio.

6.3 · Study guide: Inter-business comparison

Inter-business comparison means measuring one business against another, or against an industry average, using the same ratios. It is a powerful way to find strengths and weaknesses, but only if the two businesses are really alike. This section shows how to compare fairly and lists the problems that can make a comparison misleading.

What inter-business comparison is for

A ratio on its own is a number. A gross profit margin of 35% means little until you ask: compared with what? The two usual comparisons are with the same business last year and with another business this year. The second is inter-business comparison. It answers questions that a single business cannot answer about itself. Is our margin low because the whole trade has low margins, or because we buy badly? Do our customers pay slowly, or is 45 days normal in this industry?

Owners use the comparison to find where they are weak and to set targets. Managers use it to decide which areas need action. Banks compare loan applicants with each other and with the trade average to judge risk. Investors compare businesses to decide where to put their money. Suppliers compare customers to decide who gets credit. In every case, the comparison is useful only if the businesses are alike enough for the difference in ratios to come from performance, not from differences in size, type or accounting choices.

Comparing like with like

The golden rule is to compare like with like. Two businesses in the same trade, of similar size, with the same year end and the same accounting policies can be compared with confidence. The further apart they are on any of these points, the less the ratios tell you. A supermarket and a jeweller both sell goods, but one turns its inventory over 40 times a year on a 25% margin and the other 2 times a year on a 60% margin. Neither is doing better; they simply have different business models.

Before you compare, ask four questions. Are they in the same type of trade? Are they roughly the same size? Do they end their year on the same date? Do they use the same accounting policies? If the answer to any question is no, say so in your conclusion and explain which ratios are affected. An examiner gives credit for a comparison that recognises its own limits.

Problems of comparison: the business itself

Differences in the businesses can distort every ratio. A larger business buys in bulk and gets trade discounts, so its gross profit margin may be higher for reasons of size alone. A business that owns its premises has no rent, so its profit margin looks better than a competitor who rents, but it has far more capital employed, so its ROCE may look worse. A business that sells for cash will have almost no trade receivables, so its trade receivables turnover (days) cannot be compared with a business that gives credit.

The type of ownership matters. A sole trader works in the business and takes drawings, which are not an expense. A limited company pays its directors salaries, which are expenses. The sole trader's profit margin is therefore higher by the value of the owner's work. A company may also pay debenture interest. The age of the business matters too: an old business has old, heavily depreciated assets and a low capital employed, which raises its ROCE compared with a new business with new assets. None of these differences says anything about management skill.

Even two identical businesses can report different figures if they make different accounting choices. Depreciation is the clearest example. One business charges 20% straight-line on its vans; another charges 30% reducing balance. The same applies to the percentage used for the allowance for irrecoverable debts, to the estimate of net realisable value for slow inventory, and to whether a repair is treated as capital or revenue expenditure.

Timing matters as well. A business with a 31 January year end, just after the holiday season, will show low inventory and high cash. A one-off event in one year, such as a large irrecoverable debt or a profit on selling a building, also distorts a single year's figures. Comparing with an industry average

Trade associations and banks publish average ratios for businesses of a given type. Comparing with the average avoids the problem of choosing one competitor who may be unusual. If a pharmacy has a gross profit margin of 30% and the average is 28%, it buys or prices well. If its profit margin is 6% against an average of 8%, its expenses are high for the trade, and the owner knows where to look.

Averages have their own weaknesses. The average mixes large and small businesses, city and village, new and old. A small village pharmacy may never reach the inventory turnover of a city chain. The average may be a year or two out of date. The published averages may not be calculated in exactly the same way as the syllabus formulas, for example using opening capital for ROCE. Use the average as a starting point for questions, not as a target to be hit at any cost.

Writing a comparison answer

A full comparison answer has a clear shape. First, calculate the same ratios for both businesses, laid out side by side so that the examiner can see the pairs. Second, for each ratio or group of ratios, say which business is stronger and by how much, and give a likely reason that fits the data. Third, state the problems of the comparison that apply to these two businesses: not every problem from the list, only the ones the question gives evidence for, such as different year ends or one business renting its shop. Fourth, give a judgement that answers the question: which business should the investor choose, or which is the safer borrower, and why.

Avoid two common faults. Do not say that one business is better in every way just because it is bigger; use ratios, which remove size. Do not list limitations without linking them to the ratios they affect. A limitation earns its mark when you explain its consequence: because Business B owns its premises, its profit margin is higher than A's, so the 3 percentage point gap overstates B's advantage.

6.4 · Study guide: Interested parties

Many interested parties use a business's financial statements to make decisions, and each is looking for something different. This section covers every interested party named in the syllabus: owners, managers, employees, banks, investors and lenders, suppliers, customers, governments and tax authorities, club members, and other parties such as public and environmental bodies. It explains the decision each one is making and which statements and ratios help.

Interested parties inside and outside the business

Accounting information is prepared for people who must make decisions. Internal users are inside the business: the sole trader, the partners, the directors and the managers. They see the detailed records and can ask for any report they need. External users are outside the business: banks, investors and lenders, suppliers, customers, employees, governments and tax authorities, club members who do not sit on the committee, and other interested parties such as public and environmental bodies. They usually see only the published or summarised financial statements, and often a year after the events.

The difference matters because internal users can act on the information directly, while external users must decide whether to deal with the business at all. A manager who sees that inventory turnover is slowing can change the ordering policy tomorrow. A supplier who sees the same thing can only decide whether to give credit. In the exam, name the user, state the decision the user is making, and then say which figures help with that decision.

Owners, managers and employees

The owner of a sole trader business wants to know whether the business made a profit, whether the profit is a fair reward for the capital and the work put in, and whether drawings can safely be taken. The owner compares return on capital employed with what the money could earn elsewhere and watches the bank balance. Partners want the same, plus the fair division of profit shown in the appropriation account and current accounts. Shareholders want to know whether dividends can be paid and whether the value of their shares is growing.

Managers use the accounts to plan and control. They compare this year with last year, department with department, and actual results with the budget. They look at margins to set prices, at expense totals to control costs, and at receivables and payables to manage cash. Employees and their trade unions look at profit to judge whether the business can afford a pay rise and whether their jobs are secure. A business making losses or short of cash may have to cut staff, so employees are interested in both profitability and liquidity.

Banks, other lenders and suppliers

A bank asked for a loan wants two things: that the interest will be paid every year and that the loan will be repaid at the end. For the first, it looks at profit for the year before interest and compares it with the interest charge; it also looks at the trend of profit. For the second, it looks at the statement of financial position for assets that could be security, at existing loans, and at how the owner's capital compares with borrowing. For an overdraft, which is short-term, the bank focuses on the current and acid test (liquid) ratios and on the pattern of cash coming in.

A supplier deciding whether to sell on credit asks a narrower question: will this customer pay in 30 days? The supplier looks at the acid test (liquid) ratio, at trade payables turnover in days to see how the customer treats other suppliers, and at the bank balance or overdraft. Profit matters less; a very profitable business that holds all its wealth in inventory may still pay late. A supplier may ask for a trade reference or begin with a small credit limit.

Customers, governments, investors and lenders

Customers care about whether the business will still exist to deliver goods, honour guarantees and provide spare parts. A customer about to sign a long contract looks at liquidity and at the trend of profit to judge whether the business is a going concern. Governments and tax authorities use the reported profit for the year to assess tax, and check that expenses are genuine and that capital expenditure has not been charged as an expense. Governments also use accounts to collect statistics about the economy, to decide which businesses should receive grants, and to regulate industries such as banking or energy.

Investors and lenders, including a person thinking of buying the business, want to know the return the business earns on its capital, whether that return is rising or falling, and how risky it is. ROCE, profit margin and the amount of borrowing are the key figures. A buyer also needs information the statements do not give, such as the condition of the assets and the reason the owner is selling. Competitors are another interested party: they compare margins, expenses and turnover ratios with their own to find where they are weaker or stronger.

Club members, the public and environmental bodies

Members of a club or society pay subscriptions and elect a committee to run the club for them. They use the income and expenditure account to see whether the year ended in a surplus or a deficit, the subscriptions account to see how much is owed, and the accumulated fund to see whether the club's wealth is growing. They use this to judge whether the committee has spent their money wisely and whether subscriptions must rise.

The public and environmental bodies are interested in the effect a business has on the community and the environment. A local council may look at profit and the number of staff to judge whether a large employer is secure. An environmental group studying a chemical company looks for spending on pollution control, fines paid, and whether the company earns enough profit to pay for cleaner equipment. Much of what these parties care about, such as pollution or the quality of local jobs, is not measured in money, so they also need information from outside the financial statements.

One set of statements, many conclusions

The same statements can look good to one user and bad to another. Imagine a business with a high profit, a large bank loan and a acid test (liquid) ratio of 0.5:1. The owner is pleased with the profit. The tax authority is pleased too, because tax is based on profit. The bank that gave the loan is worried, because the business may struggle to make repayments. A supplier refuses credit because the acid test (liquid) ratio is weak. An employee may be reassured by the profit but concerned about the debt.

This is why exam questions name an interested party. Your answer must take that party's point of view and use the figures it cares about. If the question asks what a supplier would think, do not discuss ROCE at length; discuss the acid test (liquid) ratio and trade payables turnover (days). If it asks about a potential buyer, discuss the return, the trend and the risk, and then add the non-financial information the buyer would also need, such as the lease, the staff and the location.

6.5 · Study guide: Limitations of accounting statements

Financial statements and the ratios built from them are useful, but they do not tell the whole story. The syllabus names three causes of limitations: historic cost, the application of accounting policies, and non-financial aspects such as the skill of the workforce, the location of the business and the economic climate. This section explains each one, shows its effect with numbers, and tells you how to allow for it when giving advice.

Why limitations matter

Every decision in the earlier sections of this topic used the financial statements as if they were a complete and exact picture. They are not. They are a summary, prepared under rules, of transactions that have already happened, using the amounts paid at the time. When a bank, an investor or an owner relies on them without thinking, mistakes follow. A business may look highly profitable because its property is recorded at a price paid twenty years ago. Another may report a higher profit than its competitor only because it chose a lower depreciation rate.

The syllabus names three causes of limitations: historic cost, the application of accounting policies, and non-financial aspects such as the skill of the workforce, the location of the business and the economic climate. You must know each one and, more importantly, use them when giving advice. The best answers say what the figures show, then why the figures may mislead in this case, then what extra information would help. A limitation linked to the ratio it affects and the decision it could distort earns far more than a one-word list.

Historic cost

Non-current assets are recorded at the price paid when they were bought, less depreciation. Land and buildings bought years ago may now be worth far more. Because the statement of financial position shows the old cost, capital employed is understated, and return on capital employed is overstated. A business earning $36,000 on capital recorded as $200,000 shows a ROCE of 18%. If its premises are really worth $200,000 more than their carrying amount, the true capital employed is $400,000 and the true return is only 9%. Some businesses revalue land and buildings, which reduces the problem, but most assets stay at cost.

The same problem affects depreciation. A machine bought for $50,000 ten years ago is depreciated on that cost, but replacing it may cost $120,000. Profit is measured after an expense that is too low, so profit is overstated and the business may not have set aside enough to replace the machine. When prices rise, figures from different years are in dollars of different value, so even the trend of revenue may mislead. Historic cost is used because it is objective and easy to check, but the statement of financial position is not a valuation of the business.

Non-financial aspects: the workforce, location and money measurement

Only items that can be measured in money are recorded; this is the money measurement concept. It leaves out many of the things that make a business succeed or fail. The syllabus gives three examples. The skill of the workforce: trained, experienced and loyal staff produce the profit, and losing them can change everything. The location of the business: a shop on a busy street, or a factory close to its customers, has an advantage that no figure in the accounts shows. The economic climate, which is explained in the next note. Others include the quality of management, reputation, relationships with suppliers and competition.

Two businesses with identical ratios can have very different futures. One has a lease about to end and a new competitor opening across the road; the other has a long lease and a loyal customer base. The ratios cannot tell them apart. This is why a potential buyer or lender always asks questions beyond the accounts. In an exam, when asked to advise, name two or three non-financial aspects that fit the business in the question and say why each would change the decision.

Non-financial aspects: the economic climate

The economic climate is the state of the economy in which the business trades: whether it is growing or in recession, the rate of inflation, interest rates and exchange rates. None of these is recorded in the accounts, yet each changes the figures. In a recession customers spend less, so revenue and gross profit fall even if the business is run as well as before. Rising interest rates increase the cost of loans and overdrafts, which reduces profit for the year. A fall in the exchange rate makes imported goods dearer, so cost of sales rises and the gross profit margin falls unless prices can be raised.

Inflation causes a special problem when results for different years are compared. If prices in general rose by 10% and revenue rose by 6%, the business actually sold less in real terms, although the statements show growth. So when you interpret a trend, ask what was happening in the economy. A fall in ROCE during a recession may be a good result if similar businesses fell further. The statements also look backwards: they cannot show how the economy will change next year.

Application of accounting policies

Where the rules allow a choice, different businesses choose differently, and the same business may change its choice. Depreciation may be straight-line, reducing balance or revaluation, at various rates and with various estimates of useful life. The allowance for irrecoverable debts may be 2% or 5% of trade receivables. Slow-moving inventory may be kept at cost by one owner and written down to net realisable value by another. A borderline repair may be treated as revenue expenditure by one business and as capital expenditure by another.

Each choice changes profit for the year, the asset values and therefore every ratio that uses them. Two businesses with identical trading can report different profit margins and different returns on capital employed. When the question tells you that two businesses use different methods, say which figures are affected and in which direction, and suggest recalculating on the same basis. When comparing one business over time, check that its policies did not change; the consistency concept exists to limit this problem, but it does not remove it.

Estimates and the limits of ratios themselves

Applying an accounting policy often needs an estimate. Depreciation depends on a judgement about useful life and residual value, or on the rate chosen for the reducing balance method. The allowance for irrecoverable debts depends on a judgement about which customers will not pay. Inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value needs a view about future selling prices. Accruals and prepayments are sometimes estimated. So profit for the year is not an exact figure, and small differences between businesses or between years may be caused by estimates rather than by performance.

Ratios cannot remove these problems. A ratio is only as reliable as the figures in it, and it hides the size of the business: a current (working capital) ratio of 2:1 may describe $2,000 against $1,000 or $2,000,000 against $1,000,000. 7. Accounting concepts and modern practice

7.1 · Accounting concepts in practice

Separate the entity, recognise earned income and match period costs.

Business entity separates owner and business transactions. Duality records the two-sided effect. Going concern assumes the business will continue for the foreseeable future, so assets are not valued at break-up prices; historic cost records assets at their original cost. Matching or accruals charges the income and expenses of the period, whether or not cash has been received or paid; realisation recognises revenue when it is earned, normally when goods or services pass to the customer, rather than waiting for cash. Prudence avoids overstating income and assets or understating liabilities. Consistency uses comparable policies; materiality considers significance. Money measurement limits records to reliably measurable money amounts.

The owner pays a personal bill from business bank: drawings, not expense (entity). Charge depreciation consistently (consistency). Record unsold damaged inventory at lower NRV (prudence).

7.3 · Digital accounting and sustainable storage

Automation needs reliable inputs, controlled access and recoverable records.

Digital tools can capture invoices, post journals, calculate totals and produce reports quickly. Human checks remain essential for classification and unusual transactions. Manual (paper) storage is accessible without software but takes space and is vulnerable to fire, water and loss. Data storage devices such as external drives allow direct control but can fail or be lost and need protected backups; cloud services support remote access but depend on connectivity and provider security; other digital services, such as online banking records and email archives, are convenient but outside the business's full control. Not storing data safely risks loss, theft, fraud and breach of confidentiality. Use access permissions, encryption where appropriate, tested backups and retention schedules. Sustainable storage reduces unnecessary copies and resource use without destroying records that must be retained.

A business keeps encrypted working records plus a separately protected backup and tests restoration. It removes redundant copies under an agreed retention policy while retaining required evidence.

7.1 · Study guide: Accounting concepts

Accounting concepts are the agreed rules that every accountant follows when deciding what to record, how much to record and in which period. The ten concepts in the syllabus are business entity, going concern, historic cost, matching, prudence, consistency, materiality, money measurement, realisation and duality. In the exam you must name the correct concept and show how it changes a figure.

Why accountants need shared rules

Imagine three shops each choosing their own way to value inventory, record sales and charge depreciation. Their profits could not be compared, and a bank could not trust any of them. Accounting concepts solve this. They are a set of agreed rules that every accountant follows, so that financial statements from different businesses and different years are prepared on the same basis.

The concepts also protect users. An owner who wants a bank loan would like profit to look high. A rule that says revenue is only recorded when it is earned stops that owner from counting next year's orders this year. In this way the concepts make the statements reliable, comparable and fair.

The syllabus names ten concepts. You need to know what each one means, recognise it in a scenario and show its effect on a figure. Examiners rarely ask you only to define a concept. They describe a situation and expect you to name the concept, apply it and say what would go wrong if it were ignored.

The ten concepts at a glance

Keep this table in your head. The meaning column tells you the rule. The application column shows the rule changing a real figure. In the exam a good answer always contains both parts: the rule and its effect on the accounts.

Notice that several concepts work together. Going concern justifies historic cost, because a business that will keep trading does not need to show forced-sale values. Matching and realisation are both about timing: matching decides the period for expenses and realisation decides the period for revenue. Prudence limits optimism, while materiality stops accountants wasting time on tiny amounts. Learn the names exactly as written, because the examiner marks the name of the concept, not a description of it.

Entity, duality and money measurement: what goes into the books

Three concepts decide what is recorded at all. Business entity says that the owner and the business are different. When Nadia pays her own dentist from the business bank account, the business has not had an expense. It has given money to the owner, so the entry is debit drawings and credit bank. If it were recorded as an expense, profit would be too low and capital would still be too high.

Duality says every transaction has two effects, which is why double entry exists. A loan of $8,000 received increases the asset bank and increases the liability loan. The accounting equation stays balanced. When a student forgets duality and records only one side, the trial balance will not agree.

Money measurement says only items with a reliable money value are recorded. A skilled manager, a good location or a happy customer base all help the business, but nobody can measure them objectively. They are left out. This is one reason why financial statements never show the full value of a business, a limitation you studied in Topic 6.

Going concern and historic cost: how assets are valued

Going concern is an assumption. Accountants assume the business will keep trading for at least the next twelve months. Because of this, a delivery van bought for $18,000 is not shown at the $11,000 a dealer would pay for it today. The business is not selling the van. It will use it for years, so the cost is spread over those years as depreciation. If the business were about to close, going concern would no longer apply and assets would be shown at the amounts they could be sold for.

Historic cost says assets are recorded at the price actually paid. The price is on an invoice, so it is objective and can be checked. A guess about current market value is not. If premises bought for $60,000 are now worth $75,000, the $15,000 increase is not recorded by a sole trader, because the gain has not been realised and the valuation is only an opinion.

These two concepts explain the carrying amount you have calculated many times: cost less accumulated depreciation. Neither market value nor replacement cost appears in that calculation.

Matching and realisation: deciding the period

Profit for the year must compare the revenue earned in the year with the expenses used to earn it. Matching, also called the accruals concept, puts each expense in the period it relates to. If insurance of $1,200 is paid on 1 October for twelve months and the year ends on 31 December, only three months, $300, belongs to this year. The other $900 is a prepayment, shown as a current asset. Wages owed but not yet paid are added to the expense and shown as a current liability. Depreciation and the allowance for irrecoverable debts are also matching adjustments, because they put the cost of using assets and the expected cost of irrecoverable debts into the period that benefited.

Realisation decides when revenue is recognised. The rule is: when the goods or services are delivered and ownership passes, so the amount is reasonably certain. Not when the order arrives, and not when the cash arrives. A credit sale delivered on 29 December is revenue for this year, with a trade receivable at the year end, even though cash comes in January. A deposit received for goods to be delivered next year is not revenue yet. It is a liability, because the business still owes the customer the goods.

Prudence, consistency and materiality: using judgement sensibly

Prudence stops optimism. Where there is doubt, choose the figure that gives the lower profit or the lower asset value, and recognise a likely loss as soon as it is known. This is why inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value, why an allowance for irrecoverable debts is made before any customer actually fails to pay, and why a possible gain on a property is not recorded until it is sold. Prudence does not mean deliberately understating profit. That would also mislead users.

Consistency says keep the same method year after year. A business that used reducing balance depreciation last year must use it this year. If it switched methods to make profit look better, the two years could not be compared. A change is allowed only when there is a good reason, such as a new method giving a truer picture, and the change and its effect should be explained.

Materiality keeps things sensible. A $12 stapler will last several years, so in theory it is a non-current asset to be depreciated. In practice the amount is so small that nobody's decision would change, so it is charged as an expense at once. What is material depends on the size of the business: $500 matters to a market stall but not to a national chain.

How to answer a concept question in the exam

Most questions follow one pattern: a short scenario, then an instruction such as name the concept, explain how it applies, or calculate the corrected figure. Build your answer in four moves. First, name the concept exactly. Second, give its rule in one sentence. Third, apply the rule to the figures in the question, showing the amount that changes. Fourth, state the consequence of ignoring it: profit overstated or understated, an asset or liability wrong, or users misled.

Watch for concepts that sound alike. Prudence lowers a figure because of doubt; materiality simplifies a figure because it is small. Matching concerns expenses and the period they belong to; realisation concerns revenue and the moment it is earned. Historic cost is about the amount recorded; going concern is the assumption that allows that amount to be kept.

Sometimes two concepts can apply. Treating an owner's private purchase as drawings is business entity. Recording it at the invoice price is historic cost. When the question asks for one concept, choose the one that explains the decision described, not every concept that touches the transaction.

7.2 · Study guide: Ethical considerations

Accounting needs an ethical framework because many people rely on figures prepared by someone else. The five fundamental principles are integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour. The exam gives you a situation, often an owner or manager putting pressure on a book-keeper, and asks you to name the principle at risk, explain the correct response and evaluate why applying the framework matters to stakeholders and to society.

The need for an ethical framework in accounting

Financial statements are used by people who were not there when the transactions happened. A bank deciding on a loan, a supplier deciding on credit terms, a partner receiving a profit share and the tax authority all rely on figures prepared by someone else. They can only rely on those figures if the person who prepared them was honest, careful and independent. Ethics is the set of moral rules that makes this trust possible.

Professional accounting bodies publish a code of ethics that every member must follow. The code sets out five fundamental principles: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour. A member who breaks the code can be fined, suspended or removed from the profession. The framework is needed because accountants often face pressure, have access to private information and make judgements, for example about inventory values or the allowance for irrecoverable debts, that outsiders cannot easily check.

In the syllabus you need to explain why the framework is needed, know the five principles, recognise them in a scenario and explain why applying them matters to stakeholders and society. Most exam situations involve pressure: an owner wants a higher profit for a loan, a lower profit for tax, or a secret kept from a partner. Your job is to show how the accountant should respond.

The five fundamental principles

Each principle has a short meaning and a typical way it is broken. Learn the exact names, including the full phrase professional competence and due care, because an examiner cannot give the mark for a half name. The example breaches in the table show the type of situation that appears in questions: pressure from an owner, a conflict of interest, a task beyond the accountant's skill, loose talk about a client, or conduct that damages the reputation of the profession.

Sometimes more than one principle is at risk. Recording false sales to please an owner breaks integrity because the record is dishonest, and also objectivity because the accountant has let pressure replace judgement. When a question asks for the principle, give the one that best fits the behaviour described, and if two clearly apply, name both and explain each in one sentence.

Integrity and objectivity in practice

Integrity is the base of everything else. An accountant with integrity does not record a transaction that did not happen, does not leave out one that did, and does not describe a figure in a way that hides its real nature. If a manager asks for a supplier's invoice to be held back until next month so that this month's profit looks better, integrity says no. The invoice belongs to this month.

Objectivity is about independence of mind. A conflict of interest exists when the accountant, or someone close to the accountant, could gain from a decision. The classic example is choosing a supplier owned by a relative, or valuing inventory for a business in which the accountant holds shares. The correct response is to declare the conflict and let someone independent make the decision. Accepting gifts or hospitality from a client or supplier also threatens objectivity, because the accountant may feel obliged to return the favour.

Pressure from an owner is the most common threat to both principles. The accountant should explain the effect of the request, refuse to make a false entry, keep a written record and, if the pressure continues, take the matter to a more senior person or a professional body.

Competence, due care and confidentiality

Professional competence means having the knowledge the job needs. Due care means using that knowledge properly: checking calculations, meeting deadlines and following the correct standards. A book-keeper who has never prepared a manufacturing account should say so, learn how, or pass the work to someone who can. Guessing is a breach even when the guess turns out to be close. Due care also means checking your own work. A depreciation charge entered as $250 instead of $2,500 understates expenses by $2,250, and the person who made the slip is responsible for finding and correcting it.

Confidentiality covers everything learned at work: profits, customer lists, salaries, plans for expansion. The information belongs to the client or employer. It must not be passed to friends, family or competitors, and it must not be used for the accountant's own benefit, for example by buying shares before good results are announced. Confidentiality continues after the accountant leaves the job.

There are limits. Information may be disclosed when the law requires it, for example to a court or the tax authority, when the client gives permission, or when a professional body investigates. Disclosure in these cases is not a breach.

Professional behaviour and the consequences of unethical conduct

Professional behaviour means obeying laws and regulations and acting in a way that keeps the public's respect for the profession. Missing legal filing deadlines, making false claims in advertising, or behaving rudely and dishonestly with clients all damage that respect, even when no figure is changed.

The consequences of unethical behaviour fall on three groups. The accountant may lose his or her job, be fined or removed from the professional body, lose the right to practise and in serious cases face criminal charges for fraud. The business may lose the trust of its bank and suppliers, pay penalties or extra tax, have to restate its accounts, lose customers and in the worst case close. Users of the statements suffer because they make decisions on false information: a bank lends to a business that cannot repay, a partner receives too small a share of profit, an investor pays too much for shares, and employees may lose their jobs when the truth comes out.

When a question asks for consequences, name the group affected and give the specific harm. Two consequences for the accountant and two for the business is a safe pattern for a four-mark answer.

Why an ethical framework matters to stakeholders and society

Applying an ethical framework is not only about one accountant keeping a job. It matters to every stakeholder who uses the figures, and to society as a whole.

For stakeholders, honest statements mean better decisions. Owners can judge how well the business is really doing. Banks and other lenders can lend with confidence, often at lower interest. Suppliers can set sensible credit limits. Employees can trust that their wages, and the future of their jobs, are based on true results. Customers know their personal details are kept confidential. Investors can compare businesses fairly.

For society, the effects are wider. The tax authority collects the correct tax, which pays for schools, hospitals and roads. Money flows to businesses that really perform well, not to those that only look good, so jobs are more secure. Fewer frauds and sudden collapses means fewer people lose savings or jobs. Honest reporting of environmental information, such as energy use or waste, lets environmental bodies and the public hold businesses to account. When accountants act ethically, people trust financial information, which helps the whole economy work. This is what serving the public interest means.

In an evaluation answer, name the stakeholder, give the specific benefit or harm, and reach a conclusion about how significant it is.

A method for answering an ethical dilemma

Exam questions describe a dilemma and ask what the accountant should do. Work through five moves. One: state the facts and the figure involved. Two: name the principle or principles at risk and say why. Three: show the effect on the financial statements if the request were followed, with amounts, for example profit overstated by $4,200 and trade payables understated by $4,200. Four: give the correct action. Usually this is to refuse the false entry, explain the effect to the person making the request, suggest the honest alternative and keep a written note of the conversation. Five: say what to do if the pressure continues, which is to raise the matter with a more senior person, the owners, or the professional body, and to seek advice before resigning or reporting externally.

A good answer is practical. Resigning on the spot or reporting the owner to the police as a first step is not what the examiner expects. Equally, agreeing to the request and promising to fix it next year is never acceptable, because the statements for this year would be false.

How ethics and the accounting concepts work together

Many unethical requests are really requests to break an accounting concept. Recording next year's sales this year breaks realisation. Hiding a supplier invoice breaks matching. Valuing inventory at selling price breaks prudence. Treating the owner's private spending as a business expense to reduce tax breaks business entity. Changing the depreciation method every year to smooth profit breaks consistency. So when you analyse a dilemma, name both the ethical principle that the accountant would breach, usually integrity or objectivity, and the accounting concept that the figures would breach.

This also tells you how to calculate the damage. Apply the accounting concept correctly, compare the correct figure with the requested figure, and the difference is the misstatement. Adding the differences gives the total effect on profit, which is a common final step in a structured question.

Remember that the aim of ethics is not to make profit lower. It is to make the statements true. An accountant who deliberately understates profit to help an owner pay less tax is just as unethical as one who overstates it for a loan.

7.3 · Study guide: Technology and sustainability

Many businesses now keep all or part of their accounting records using digital applications. Whatever method is used, accounting data must be stored safely, so that it stays available, accurate and private, and sustainably, so that storage does not waste paper, energy or equipment and records are kept only for as long as they are needed. You must explain the risks of poor storage and compare the advantages and disadvantages of manual storage, data storage devices, cloud services and other digital services.

Using digital applications for all or part of the records

A business does not have to choose between keeping everything by hand and keeping everything on a computer. Many small businesses use digital applications for part of their records only. A trader may keep a handwritten cash book but issue sales invoices from an app on a phone. Another may keep paper ledgers but use online banking for bank statements and a spreadsheet for the petty cash book. A larger business may keep all its books of prime entry, ledgers and financial statements digitally.

The accounting does not change. Every transaction still has a debit and a credit, and the same concepts apply. What changes is speed and effort: totals are calculated automatically, documents are produced quickly, records take no shelf space and can be shared. The limitations are the cost of equipment, the need for training, dependence on power and internet, and the fact that a wrong amount typed in is processed without question. When records are split between manual and digital parts, items can also be missed when copied from one to the other.

You do not need to know any particular software package. Examiners reward general points applied to the business in the question.

Why accounting data must be stored safely

Storing data safely means three things: the data is available when it is needed, it is accurate and has not been changed without permission, and it is private, so only authorised people can see it.

There are several reasons. The business needs its records to prepare the statement of profit or loss and the statement of financial position, to know who owes it money and whom it must pay, and to make decisions. The tax authority and the law usually require records to be kept for a number of years, and penalties can follow if they cannot be produced. Records are evidence if a customer or supplier disputes an amount. Finally, accounting data contains personal and confidential details of customers, suppliers and employees. Losing or exposing that data breaks the ethical principle of confidentiality you met in 7.2 and may break data protection law.

Why accounting data must be stored sustainably

Sustainability means meeting today's needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Storing records has an environmental cost. Paper uses trees, water and energy, and printing every invoice, statement and payment confirmation creates waste. Digital storage is not free of impact either: computers and the large data centres that run cloud services use electricity day and night, and old devices become electronic waste that can release harmful materials if dumped.

A business stores data sustainably when it prints only what is really needed, sends invoices and statements electronically, avoids keeping many unnecessary copies, switches equipment off when not in use, chooses energy-efficient equipment and providers, keeps records for the required retention period and then destroys them securely, and reuses or recycles old devices through approved services.

Safety and sustainability must be balanced. Deleting records too early to save space breaks the law, and having no backup to save energy is unsafe. The aim is enough copies, kept for long enough, and no more. Sustainable storage often saves money too, and it improves the business's reputation with customers and environmental bodies.

Manual storage and data storage devices

Manual storage means paper records: handwritten books of prime entry and ledgers, and paper source documents kept in files, boxes or cabinets. Its advantages are that it needs no power, equipment or internet, it cannot be hacked over the internet, it is simple for a very small business, and the original documents are available as evidence. Its disadvantages are that it takes up space, is slow to search, is easily destroyed by fire, flood or theft, usually exists as a single copy, is hard to share with an accountant in another place, and uses large amounts of paper.

Data storage devices hold digital records on equipment the business owns: the hard drive inside a computer, an external hard drive, a USB flash drive or a memory card. Their advantages are that the business controls its own data, the records can be reached without internet, devices are cheap to buy once and hold large amounts of data, and a portable device can be used to keep a backup copy away from the premises. Their disadvantages are that devices can fail without warning, small devices are easily lost or stolen, a virus can spread to them, the business must remember to make its own backups, and old devices must be wiped and disposed of responsibly.

Cloud services and other digital services

A cloud service stores the business's data on a provider's servers, reached through the internet. Its advantages are that records can be reached from any place and by several users at once, including the owner at home and an accountant in another town; the provider usually makes regular backups; the data is safe from a fire or theft at the business's premises; and the business does not need to buy and maintain its own storage equipment. Its disadvantages are that nothing can be reached without an internet connection, there is usually a continuing subscription, the business relies on the provider's security and on the provider staying in business, the data may be held in another country, and anyone who learns the login details can reach the data from anywhere. Data centres also use a great deal of energy, although sharing large efficient centres can use less energy than many small office servers.

Other digital services also hold accounting data: online banking, email, card payment providers and electronic invoicing services. They create records automatically, such as bank statements and payment confirmations, and reduce paper. However, the business does not control them, its data becomes spread across many places, a provider may keep only a limited history, and false emails can be used to steal login details. Important records from these services should be downloaded and kept for the retention period.

Comparing the four types of storage

No single type of storage is best for every business. A small market trader with a few paper invoices may manage well with manual records in a locked, fireproof cabinet. A business with several users or locations gains most from a cloud service. Many businesses use a combination: a cloud service or office computer for the main records, a backup kept in a different place, online banking for bank statements, and paper originals of key documents for as long as the law requires. When you advise a business, match the storage to its size, number of users and locations, the reliability of its internet, the sensitivity of its data and how long it must keep records.

Keeping data safe and storage sustainable in practice

Most good practice can be learned as a measure and the risk it reduces. Regular backups, kept on a separate device or in the cloud and away from the premises, and tested so that they can be restored, protect against loss. Strong individual passwords, two-step login and cancelling the logins of staff who leave protect against unauthorised access. Access levels, so that the person who enters invoices cannot also change a supplier's bank details, protect against fraud. Anti-virus software and regular updates protect against viruses. Encryption protects data on a laptop or drive that is stolen. Physical security, such as locked rooms and fireproof cabinets raised off the floor, protects paper records and equipment. Staff training reduces the chance of someone opening a false email or sharing a password.

For sustainability: print only when needed, send documents electronically, keep a sensible number of copies, switch equipment off, follow a written retention schedule, shred paper and recycle it after the retention period, and wipe old devices before reusing or recycling them.
