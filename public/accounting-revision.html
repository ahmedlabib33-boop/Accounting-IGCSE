1. Accounting foundations

1.1 · Why accounting matters

Profit is not the same as cash.

Book-keeping records transactions systematically. Accounting classifies, summarises and interprets those records to help decisions. A business can earn profit on credit while lacking cash to pay suppliers. Records help an owner monitor performance, control resources and plan finance. A statement of profit or loss measures performance over a period; a statement of financial position shows resources and obligations on a date.

A trader sells goods costing $600 for $900 on credit. Gross profit is $300, but no cash has yet arrived. The customer owes $900.
Check: Goods costing $600 are sold on credit for $900. What is the immediate gross profit?
  1. $300
  2. $900
  3. $600
  4. $0

Answer A: Revenue $900 less cost of sales $600 gives gross profit $300. Credit terms affect collection, not this profit calculation.

Check: Which activity goes beyond book-keeping?
  1. Filing a receipt
  2. Interpreting profitability for a decision
  3. Recording an invoice
  4. Entering a bank payment

Answer B: Accounting includes interpretation and decision support, while book-keeping concentrates on systematic recording.

1.2 · The accounting equation

Assets = liabilities + owner equity

Assets are resources controlled by a business, such as inventory, equipment and receivables. Liabilities are amounts owed, such as payables and loans. Equity is the owner claim after liabilities. Each transaction preserves the equation. Capital introduced increases equity; drawings reduce it; profit increases it and loss reduces it.

Assets $48,000 and liabilities $13,000 give equity $35,000. Paying a supplier $2,000 reduces both bank and payables; equity stays $35,000.
Check: Assets are $48,000 and liabilities $13,000. Calculate equity.
  1. $48,000
  2. $35,000
  3. $61,000
  4. $13,000

Answer B: Subtract liabilities from assets: 48,000 − 13,000 = 35,000.

Check: Equity is $18,000 and liabilities $7,000. Assets?
  1. $18,000
  2. $7,000
  3. $25,000
  4. $11,000

Answer C: Assets = equity + liabilities = 18,000 + 7,000.

2. Recording transactions

2.1 · Debit and credit entries

Every transaction has equal total debits and credits.

Debit increases assets, expenses and drawings. Credit increases liabilities, income and capital. Reverse these entries for decreases. Credit purchases of goods for resale debit purchases and credit the supplier; credit sales debit the customer and credit sales. Do not confuse the bank statement perspective with the business ledger perspective.

Buy goods for resale on credit from Noor for $750: debit purchases $750; credit Noor $750. Pay Noor later: debit Noor, credit bank.
Check: Which entry records goods for resale purchased on credit?
  1. Debit bank; credit sales
  2. Debit equipment; credit bank
  3. Debit purchases; credit supplier
  4. Debit supplier; credit purchases

Answer C: Purchases increase on the debit side, and the amount owed to the supplier increases on the credit side.

Check: A business receives a $4,000 bank loan. Entry?
  1. Debit loan; credit bank
  2. Debit purchases; credit loan
  3. Debit bank; credit revenue
  4. Debit bank; credit loan

Answer D: Bank assets and loan liabilities both increase.

2.1 · Ledger balances and running balances

Balance carried down closes a period; balance brought down opens the next.

A T account places debits on the left and credits on the right. Insert the difference on the smaller side to balance totals. Carry the balance forward on the opposite side. Asset and expense balances normally debit; liabilities, capital and income normally credit. A running-balance account updates the debit or credit balance after every transaction. Transfer revenue and expense balances to the statement of profit or loss; carry assets and liabilities into the next period.

Bank starts debit $1,200, receives $500 and pays $300. Closing debit balance = 1,200 + 500 − 300 = $1,400.
Check: Bank opens with debit $1,200, receives $500 and pays $300. Closing balance?
  1. $1,400 credit
  2. $2,000 debit
  3. $400 debit
  4. $1,400 debit

Answer D: An asset balance rises with receipts and falls with payments: 1,200 + 500 − 300 = 1,400 debit.

2.1 · Ledger divisions and digital records

Personal accounts support control; general accounts classify activity.

The sales ledger holds individual credit customers. The purchases ledger holds individual credit suppliers. The nominal or general ledger holds other accounts such as capital, expenses and assets. Digital ledgers can post automatically and produce balances quickly, but incorrect source data or mapping still produces incorrect records. Access controls, audit trails and reconciliations remain necessary.

A customer invoice updates that customer in the sales ledger and the sales account in the general ledger. A supplier invoice updates the purchases ledger.
Check: Where is an individual credit customer account kept?
  1. Sales ledger
  2. Purchases ledger
  3. Petty cash book
  4. Inventory list

Answer A: The sales ledger contains individual trade receivable accounts.

2.1 · Posting a complete transaction sequence

Keep ledger sides consistent across the whole sequence.

Start by identifying accounts, then the nature of each account, then whether it increases or decreases. Post each entry to both accounts. When an expense account closes, transfer its debit balance to profit or loss. For a revenue account transfer its credit balance. Financial-position balances carry forward rather than being closed as expenses. A digital running-balance layout gives date, details, debit, credit and a balance after each transaction.

Owner invests $5,000: bank debit, capital credit. Buy equipment $1,200 cash: equipment debit, bank credit. Pay rent $300: rent debit, bank credit. Closing bank debit $3,500; equipment debit $1,200; rent debit $300; capital credit $5,000. Debits equal credits.
Check: After investment $5,000, equipment cash purchase $1,200 and rent payment $300, bank balance?
  1. $1,500 credit
  2. $3,500 debit
  3. $6,500 debit
  4. $3,800 debit

Answer B: Bank = 5,000 − 1,200 − 300 = 3,500 debit.

2.2 · Business source documents

Record from evidence; distinguish a transaction from a summary.

An invoice gives details of a sale or purchase on credit. A credit note reduces an invoiced amount, for example for returned goods. A debit note can request a correction or notify a charge; check who issued it and why. A receipt confirms payment; a cheque counterfoil records a cheque issued; a paying-in slip supports a bank deposit. A bank statement lists bank movements, while a statement of account summarises transactions and the balance with a customer. Documents can be paper or digital; keep retrievable records.

Goods worth $80 are returned by a customer. The seller issues a credit note and records sales returns, reducing the customer balance.
Check: Which document does a seller issue to reduce a customer invoice for returned goods?
  1. Cheque counterfoil
  2. Statement of financial position
  3. Credit note
  4. Paying-in slip

Answer C: A credit note provides evidence of the reduction in the customer debt.

Check: Which document summarises a customer account over a period?
  1. Statement of account
  2. Paying-in slip
  3. Individual receipt
  4. Petty cash voucher

Answer A: A statement of account summarises transactions and the outstanding balance.

2.3 · Books of prime entry

Credit goods transactions use journals; cash movements use the cash book.

The sales journal records credit sales of goods; purchases journal records credit purchases of goods. Sales returns and purchases returns journals record the corresponding returns. Cash sales and cash purchases go in the cash book. The general journal records entries such as corrections, depreciation, opening entries and credit purchases of non-current assets. Journal totals post to general accounts, and individual invoices post to personal accounts.

Buy a delivery van on credit for $12,000. Use the general journal: debit motor vehicles and credit the supplier. It is not a purchase of resale goods.
Check: A van is acquired on credit. Which book normally records the entry first?
  1. Purchases journal for goods
  2. Sales returns journal
  3. Petty cash book
  4. General journal

Answer D: The purchases journal is for goods bought on credit for resale; a non-current asset is recorded through the general journal.

Check: A petty-cash float is $200 and expenditure $65. Cash remaining?
  1. $200
  2. $135
  3. $265
  4. $65

Answer B: 200 − 65 = 135.

2.3 · Cash book and discounts

Trade discount reduces invoice value; cash discount rewards prompt payment.

The cash book is both a prime-entry book and the ledger for bank and cash. Cash and bank receipts are debit entries; payments are credit entries. A transfer between cash and bank is a contra. An overdraft is a credit bank balance; physical cash cannot be overdrawn. Record card receipts and online transfers in bank when appropriate. Trade discount is deducted before recording; cash discount allowed is an expense and cash discount received is income.

List price $1,000 less 10% trade discount gives invoice $900. A 2% prompt-payment discount on $900 is $18, so cash paid is $882.
Check: A $1,000 list price has 10% trade discount then 2% cash discount. Payment?
  1. $882
  2. $880
  3. $900
  4. $980

Answer A: Invoice = 1,000 × 90% = 900. Payment = 900 × 98% = 882; the discounts do not simply add.

2.3 · Petty cash and recording choices

Imprest reimbursement restores the authorised float.

Petty cash pays small expenses supported by vouchers. Analyse expenditure into columns such as postage and travel. With an imprest, reimbursement equals the amount spent if there are no discrepancies. Keeping cash is convenient for small purchases but increases theft and handling risk. Manual books are inexpensive and usable without power but are slower to summarise. Digital entry speeds calculations and searching, but needs training, backups and checks against duplication.

An imprest of $150 has vouchers totalling $112. Cash remaining is $38; reimburse $112 to restore $150.
Check: An imprest is $150 and cash remaining is $38, with complete vouchers. Reimbursement?
  1. $188
  2. $112
  3. $38
  4. $150

Answer B: The reimbursement replaces expenditure: 150 − 38 = 112.

2.3 · Returns, discounts and cash-book posting

Record the net trade invoice first; recognise settlement discount when taken.

For credit sales, debit the customer and credit sales at invoice value after trade discount. A return debits sales returns and credits the customer. A cash discount allowed debits discount allowed and credits the customer as part of settlement; bank receives the cash amount. Reverse the perspective for purchases: returns and discount received reduce the supplier amount. A cash-book discount column is totalled and posted to the appropriate discount account, not balanced as a bank asset.

Invoice $1,000 less trade discount 10% = $900. Customer returns goods invoiced at $100: receivable $800. A 2% settlement discount is $16; bank receipt $784 clears $800 due.
Check: Invoice after trade discount $900, returns $100, then 2% discount on the balance. Receipt?
  1. $800
  2. $782
  3. $784
  4. $882

Answer C: (900 − 100) × 0.98 = 784.

3. Checking records

3.1 · Trial balance and its limitations

Equal debit and credit totals do not prove that every entry is correct.

A trial balance lists ledger debit and credit balances to test arithmetic agreement. Asset, expense and drawings balances usually appear as debits; liabilities, equity and revenue as credits. It can reveal one-sided or unequal postings. It cannot reveal complete omission, errors of original entry, commission or principle, complete reversal, or equal compensating errors. Prepare it from balances, not all ledger transaction totals.

A $300 sale is completely omitted. Both debit receivables and credit sales are missing; the trial balance still agrees.
Check: Which error can leave the trial balance totals equal?
  1. Posting only the debit of a sale
  2. Adding $200 to only one total
  3. Posting unequal debit and credit amounts
  4. Complete omission of a credit sale

Answer D: Complete omission removes both equal entries, so totals may still agree.

Check: Buying equipment but debiting purchases is an error of:
  1. Trial-balance addition only
  2. Cash shortage
  3. Principle
  4. One-sided posting

Answer C: A non-current asset has been classified as a revenue item.

3.1 · Recognising errors that balance

A balanced trial balance tests equality, not classification or completeness.

Omission leaves out both entries. Original-entry error uses the wrong amount in both accounts. Commission uses the wrong account of the same class, such as the wrong customer. Principle uses the wrong class, such as purchases instead of equipment. Complete reversal puts both entries on the opposite sides. Compensating errors cancel numerically. Explain the actual mistaken entries when identifying the type; a balanced total does not tell you which type occurred.

A $250 invoice to Salma is debited to Sara and credited correctly to sales. Both customers are receivable accounts: error of commission. Total receivables and total trial-balance debits remain unchanged.
Check: Invoice to Salma posted to Sara, both customers. Error type?
  1. Commission
  2. Principle
  3. Complete omission
  4. Complete reversal

Answer A: The wrong account of the same class was used.

3.2 · Correction journals and suspense

Use suspense only where an imbalance needs a temporary balancing entry.

Correct an error by removing the wrong effect and recording the required effect. A purchases entry posted to equipment is an error of principle and needs a transfer, not suspense. A one-sided entry may require suspense. If debit balances exceed credits, suspense initially has a credit balance. Calculate how a correction changes expenses, income, assets and profit rather than assuming every correction affects profit.

Repairs $400 were debited to equipment. Correct with debit repairs $400 and credit equipment $400. Profit and equipment each fall $400 before any related depreciation adjustment.
Check: Repairs $400 were treated as equipment, with no depreciation charged. Correcting this makes profit:
  1. $800 higher
  2. $400 lower
  3. $400 higher
  4. Unchanged

Answer B: The omitted expense must be charged, reducing profit by 400 and the asset by 400.

Check: A $100 payment is credited to bank correctly but omitted from expenses. Correction?
  1. Debit suspense; credit expense $100
  2. Debit bank; credit expense $100
  3. No correction needed
  4. Debit expense; credit suspense $100

Answer D: The missing debit is to expense; suspense supplies the matching credit.

3.2 · Preparing a suspense account

Analyse the imbalance before choosing the suspense side.

If debit balances total less than credit balances, insert a debit suspense balance for the difference. Correct each error with a complete journal, using suspense only for the missing or incorrect balancing effect. An entry posted to the wrong side often requires twice the original amount to correct. A zero suspense balance confirms the identified imbalance is removed, but does not prove all balanced errors are corrected.

A $200 rent payment is correctly credited to bank but credited to rent instead of debited. Debits are $400 short. Suspense opens debit $400. Correct with debit rent $400 and credit suspense $400; this reverses the wrong credit and creates the required debit.
Check: Rent $200 was credited instead of debited; bank is correct. Debit needed to correct rent?
  1. $100
  2. $0
  3. $400
  4. $200

Answer C: Reverse the wrong 200 credit and add the missing 200 debit: total correcting debit 400.

3.3 · Cash book update and bank reconciliation

Update the cash book first; reconcile timing differences second.

Use the bank statement to record bank charges, interest, direct debits, standing orders, transfers and cash-book errors not yet entered. Unpresented cheques and uncredited deposits are timing differences and do not need another cash-book entry. Starting with a positive bank-statement balance, add uncredited deposits and subtract unpresented cheques to reach the updated cash-book balance. Treat overdrafts with signed numbers. Bank errors stay in reconciliation until the bank corrects them. Digital payments may reduce delays but do not eliminate errors.

Cash book $2,000 omits $50 charges: updated balance $1,950. Statement $1,700 plus deposits $400 less cheques $150 also equals $1,950.
Check: Cash book debit $2,000 omits $50 bank charges. Updated balance?
  1. $2,050 debit
  2. $1,950 credit
  3. $2,000 debit
  4. $1,950 debit

Answer D: Debit bank-charges expense and credit bank 50, reducing the bank asset to 1,950.

Check: Positive bank-statement balance $1,000, uncredited deposit $300, unpresented cheque $200. Updated cash-book asset balance?
  1. $1,100
  2. $900
  3. $1,500
  4. $500

Answer A: Using a positive statement balance: 1,000 + 300 − 200 = 1,100.

3.3 · Bank overdrafts and bank errors

Use signed balances to avoid reversing overdraft adjustments.

A bank-statement overdraft is negative from the business perspective. Starting there, add uncredited deposits and subtract unpresented cheques. A bank error is adjusted in the reconciliation while awaiting correction, not silently posted as a real transaction in the cash book. A direct debit is a genuine payment and updates the cash book if omitted. Clearly label the starting balance because adjustment directions depend on where the reconciliation starts.

Statement overdraft $600 is −600. Uncredited deposit $900 and unpresented cheque $200 give −600 + 900 − 200 = +100, an updated cash-book debit balance $100.
Check: Statement overdraft $600, deposit outstanding $900 and cheque outstanding $200. Updated bank balance?
  1. $100 debit
  2. $100 credit
  3. $1,700 debit
  4. $700 credit

Answer A: Signed calculation: −600 + 900 − 200 = +100.

3.4 · Sales ledger control account

Opening receivables + credit sales − settlements and reductions = closing receivables.

The receivables control summarises the sales ledger. Debit credit sales, dishonoured receipts, interest charged and refunds of customer credit balances. Credit receipts, discount allowed, sales returns, irrecoverable debts and contra transfers. Credit customer balances can appear separately from debit balances. Source credit sales from the sales journal and receipts from the cash book. Automated control totals still need review for omitted or duplicate entries.

Opening receivables $4,000 + credit sales $8,000 − receipts $7,000 − returns $300 − discount $100 = closing receivables $4,600.
Check: Using opening $4,000, sales $8,000, receipts $7,000, returns $300, discounts $100, closing receivables?
  1. $12,000
  2. $4,600
  3. $5,400
  4. $3,600

Answer B: 4,000 + 8,000 − 7,000 − 300 − 100 = 4,600.

Check: Discount received appears on which side of payables control?
  1. Neither because it has no effect
  2. Debit
  3. Credit
  4. Both as a cash receipt

Answer B: Discount received reduces the amount owed, so debit payables control.

3.4 · Purchases ledger control account

Opening payables + credit purchases − settlements and reductions = closing payables.

The payables control normally has a credit balance. Credit credit purchases, interest charged by suppliers and refunds received from suppliers when relevant. Debit payments, discount received, purchases returns and contra transfers against receivables. Debit supplier balances can be shown separately. A contra debits payables control and credits receivables control because both obligations reduce. Use prime-entry totals, not cash purchases, to calculate credit purchases.

Opening payables $3,000 + purchases $6,000 − payments $5,000 − returns $200 − discount $100 = closing payables $3,700.
Check: The business owes a supplier $500 and is owed $200 by that same business. Contra reduces payables by:
  1. $700
  2. $300
  3. $200
  4. $500

Answer C: Offset the smaller reciprocal balance, 200: debit payables control and credit receivables control.

3.4 · Unusual control-account entries

A refund and a dishonoured receipt reverse different previous effects.

If a customer overpays, its personal account may carry a credit balance. Refunding that overpayment debits receivables control and credits bank. A dishonoured customer cheque restores the debt: debit customer, credit bank. Supplier overpayments can create debit payables balances; refunds from suppliers credit payables control and debit bank. Interest on an overdue customer account debits receivables and credits interest income. Control accounts summarise totals and help locate discrepancies but cannot prevent every error.

A customer cheque $300 previously reduced receivables. It is dishonoured: debit customer $300 and credit bank $300, restoring the debt.
Check: A customer cheque is dishonoured. Effect on receivables control?
  1. Credit reduces the customer debt
  2. No effect
  3. Debit decreases bank only
  4. Debit increases the customer debt

Answer D: The previously settled debt must be reinstated.

4. Accounting adjustments

4.1 · Capital and revenue items

Capital expenditure creates or improves a non-current asset; routine running costs are revenue.

The cost of an asset includes expenditure needed to bring it into intended use, such as installation. Routine repairs, fuel and insurance are expenses. Capital receipts include owner investment, loans and asset-sale proceeds; revenue receipts arise from normal income. Treating an expense as an asset overstates assets and profit; treating an asset as an expense understates both before depreciation effects. Classify by substance, not the size of payment.

Machine $5,000 plus installation $500 has capital cost $5,500. A later service costing $200 is an expense.
Check: Machine $5,000, installation $500 and routine service $200. Initial capital cost?
  1. $5,500
  2. $5,700
  3. $5,000
  4. $500

Answer A: Installation is needed to put the machine into use. Routine servicing is a revenue expense.

Check: Routine repair costs incorrectly capitalised cause, before depreciation effects:
  1. Profit lower and assets higher
  2. No effect on either
  3. Profit and assets to be overstated
  4. Profit and assets to be understated

Answer C: The expense is omitted and an asset wrongly increased.

4.1 · Capital receipts and revenue receipts

Receiving cash does not necessarily create revenue.

Revenue receipts arise from trading or other income, such as service fees. Capital receipts include new owner capital, loans and proceeds from selling non-current assets. A loan increases cash and liabilities without increasing profit. An asset disposal may create a gain or loss, but its entire proceeds are not trading sales. Distinguish improving capacity from maintaining existing capacity when deciding whether spending creates an asset.

A business receives $10,000 from a loan and $2,000 from service fees. Cash increases $12,000, but income from these receipts is $2,000.
Check: A new bank loan $10,000 is received. Immediate profit effect?
  1. $20,000 increase
  2. $0 increase
  3. $10,000 increase
  4. $10,000 decrease

Answer B: The loan is a liability, not revenue.

4.2 · Depreciation methods

Depreciation allocates cost over useful life; it is not an asset valuation exercise.

Straight-line depreciation uses cost less expected residual value divided by useful life, or a stated percentage of cost. Reducing balance applies a rate to carrying amount. Revaluation depreciation for suitable small assets uses opening value plus additions less closing valuation. Debit depreciation expense and credit accumulated depreciation. Choose a method reflecting use: an even service pattern may fit straight line; loose tools may fit revaluation. Depreciation reduces profit without a current cash payment.

Equipment $10,000, residual value $1,000, life 3 years: annual straight-line charge $3,000. At 20% reducing balance with no residual calculation, first-year charge $2,000; second $1,600.
Check: Cost $10,000, residual $1,000, useful life 3 years. Annual straight-line depreciation?
  1. $2,000
  2. $9,000
  3. $3,000
  4. $3,333.33

Answer C: (10,000 − 1,000) ÷ 3 = 3,000.

Check: Opening tools $900, purchases $300, closing tools valuation $800. Revaluation depreciation?
  1. $200
  2. $1,200
  3. $100
  4. $400

Answer D: 900 + 300 − 800 = 400.

4.2 · Asset purchases and disposal accounts

Disposal result = proceeds − carrying amount at disposal.

Record a purchase by debiting the asset cost account and crediting bank or the supplier. On disposal transfer asset cost to the debit of disposal and accumulated depreciation to its credit. Credit disposal with proceeds and debit bank. Transfer the remaining balance to profit or loss: proceeds above carrying amount produce profit, and proceeds below it produce loss. Charge any depreciation required up to disposal under the stated policy.

Cost $8,000, accumulated depreciation $5,000 and sale proceeds $2,500. Carrying amount $3,000, so loss $500.
Check: An asset cost $8,000; accumulated depreciation $5,000; proceeds $2,500. Result?
  1. $500 profit
  2. $5,500 loss
  3. $2,500 profit
  4. $500 loss

Answer D: Carrying amount = 8,000 − 5,000 = 3,000. Proceeds are 500 below carrying amount.

4.2 · Recording disposal in three ledger accounts

Remove both the historical cost and its accumulated depreciation.

Debit disposal and credit asset cost for original cost. Debit accumulated depreciation and credit disposal for accumulated charges. Debit bank and credit disposal for sale proceeds. If disposal debits exceed credits there is a loss: debit profit or loss and credit disposal. If credits exceed debits there is a gain: debit disposal and credit profit or loss. Remove the disposed asset from future depreciation calculations.

Cost $6,000; depreciation $4,500; proceeds $1,800. Disposal debit $6,000; credits $4,500 and $1,800 = $6,300. Debit disposal $300 and credit profit or loss $300 to close the gain.
Check: Cost $6,000, accumulated depreciation $4,500, proceeds $1,800. Disposal result?
  1. $300 gain
  2. $300 loss
  3. $1,800 gain
  4. $4,200 loss

Answer A: Carrying amount = 1,500; proceeds exceed it by 300.

4.3 · Accrued and prepaid expenses

Expense = cash paid + closing accrual − opening accrual + opening prepayment − closing prepayment.

Charge the expense belonging to the period, regardless of payment date. A closing accrual is a current liability and adds to the expense. A closing prepayment is a current asset and reduces the expense. Record closing accrual by debiting expense and crediting an accrual account; record closing prepayment by debiting prepayment and crediting expense. Reverse or bring forward the balances into the next period consistently.

Rent paid $12,000 includes $2,000 for next year and there is no opening prepayment or accrual. This year expense is $10,000; current asset $2,000.
Check: Rent paid $12,000 includes $2,000 for next year, with no opening adjustment. Expense?
  1. $2,000
  2. $10,000
  3. $14,000
  4. $12,000

Answer B: Exclude the future-period amount: 12,000 − 2,000 = 10,000.

Check: Insurance expense $1,200 is unpaid at year-end. It is:
  1. An expense and current liability
  2. A prepayment asset
  3. Drawings
  4. Capital income

Answer A: The cost belongs to this year, with an amount still payable.

4.3 · Accrued and prepaid income

Income = cash received + closing receivable − opening receivable + opening income in advance − closing income in advance.

Earned income not yet received is a current asset. Cash received before earning the income is a current liability. Debit income receivable and credit income for an accrued amount. Debit income and credit income in advance for an unearned receipt. The same matching principle applies to rent received, commission and interest.

Commission received $3,000 and another $400 earned but not received, with no opening balances: income $3,400 and income receivable $400.
Check: Commission received $3,000; $400 earned remains unpaid; no opening balances. Income?
  1. $3,000
  2. $400
  3. $3,400
  4. $2,600

Answer C: Add income earned but not yet collected: 3,000 + 400 = 3,400.

4.3 · Adjustments with opening balances

Opening balances prevent this year payments being mistaken for this year expense.

For expenses, subtract an opening accrual because its payment belongs to last year. Add an opening prepayment because that amount now belongs to this year. Reverse those directions for corresponding income adjustments. Show a working table with opening amount, cash paid or received, closing amount and the matched annual total. The closing adjustment also appears as an asset or liability in financial position.

Electricity cash paid $2,500 includes last year accrual $200. Current year unpaid $350. Expense = 2,500 − 200 + 350 = $2,650.
Check: Electricity paid $2,500; opening accrual $200; closing accrual $350. Annual expense?
  1. $3,050
  2. $2,350
  3. $2,500
  4. $2,650

Answer D: Deduct the old liability and add the new one: 2,500 − 200 + 350 = 2,650.

4.4 · Irrecoverable debts and recovery

Write off a specific uncollectable amount before calculating an allowance.

An irrecoverable debt is no longer expected to be collected. Debit irrecoverable-debt expense and credit the customer. If money is later recovered, recognise the recovery as income and the receipt as bank; records must show that the old amount had been written off. Do not leave a known uncollectable balance among current assets.

Receivables $10,000 include a debt of $600 that cannot be collected. Write off $600; remaining receivables $9,400. A later recovery $200 increases bank and income.
Check: Receivables $10,000 include an irrecoverable $600. Receivables before a general allowance?
  1. $9,400
  2. $10,600
  3. $10,000
  4. $600

Answer A: The specific debt is removed first: 10,000 − 600 = 9,400.

Check: An allowance falls from $800 to $500. Effect on profit, other things equal?
  1. $800 increase
  2. $300 increase
  3. $300 decrease
  4. $500 decrease

Answer B: The reduction reverses part of the previous estimated expense.

4.4 · Allowance for irrecoverable debts

Expense the change in allowance, not the full closing allowance every year.

An allowance estimates losses on remaining receivables. Deduct it from gross receivables in the statement of financial position. Creation or increase debits expense and credits allowance; a decrease debits allowance and credits the expense adjustment. Calculate it after specific write-offs. This recognises collection uncertainty without pretending to identify every customer who will default.

After write-offs, receivables $20,000 need a 5% allowance: $1,000. Existing allowance $700 means an increase charged to profit of $300; net receivables $19,000.
Check: Receivables $20,000; required allowance 5%; existing allowance $700. Additional expense?
  1. $19,000
  2. $300
  3. $1,000
  4. $700

Answer B: Required closing allowance 1,000 less existing 700 = increase 300.

4.4 · Recovering a debt previously written off

A recovery is not a new sale.

Where a debt has already been removed, record the recovery without restoring sales revenue. A clear two-step method reinstates the customer by debiting the customer and crediting irrecoverable debts recovered, then debits bank and credits the customer for the payment. The net effect is debit bank and credit recovery income. Do not leave the recovered amount as an outstanding receivable.

A debt written off last year is recovered for $240. Net journal: bank debit $240 and recovery income credit $240.
Check: Recovery $240 of a debt written off last year is credited to:
  1. Purchases
  2. Drawings
  3. Irrecoverable debts recovered income
  4. Sales as a new invoice

Answer C: It is a recovery of a previously expensed debt, not a new sale.

4.5 · Inventory valuation and profit effects

Use the lower of cost and net realisable value for each inventory item.

Cost includes expenditure to bring inventory to its present location and condition. Net realisable value is expected selling price less completion and selling costs. Compare item by item, not by offsetting gains on one item against losses on another. Closing inventory reduces cost of sales and is a current asset. Overstated closing inventory overstates gross profit, profit, assets and equity; the opening error affects the following period in the opposite direction.

Item A costs $500 and can sell for $460 with selling costs $20. NRV $440 is lower, so record $440 and recognise a $60 reduction.
Check: Inventory cost $500, expected selling price $460 and selling costs $20. Value?
  1. $500
  2. $460
  3. $480
  4. $440

Answer D: NRV is 460 − 20 = 440; choose the lower of cost 500 and NRV 440.

Check: Closing inventory is overstated by $400. This year gross profit is:
  1. Unaffected
  2. $800 understated
  3. $400 overstated
  4. $400 understated

Answer C: Closing inventory is deducted in cost of sales; overstating it understates cost of sales.

4.5 · Inventory item-by-item comparison

Apply the lower value separately to each item.

For each inventory line calculate cost and NRV; choose the lower, then total chosen amounts. Do not compare only total cost with total NRV because one item gain must not offset another item loss. Opening inventory errors affect this year cost of sales; closing inventory errors affect both this year profit and current assets. Understatement has the opposite effect from overstatement.

Item A cost $100 and NRV $80; item B cost $100 and NRV $130. Inventory is $80 + $100 = $180, rather than lower of total cost $200 and total NRV $210.
Check: Two items: cost/NRV $100/$80 and $100/$130. Inventory total?
  1. $180
  2. $200
  3. $210
  4. $230

Answer A: Choose 80 for A and 100 for B, then sum to 180.

5. Financial statements

5.1 · Sole trader profit or loss

Cost of sales = opening inventory + net purchases + carriage inwards − closing inventory.

A sole trader owns and controls the business, keeps profits and normally has unlimited liability. Trading businesses sell goods; service businesses earn fees; manufacturers produce goods; one business can combine these activities. For a trader calculate net revenue after sales returns, then gross profit, add other income and subtract period expenses. Service statements normally start with fee income. Adjust for depreciation, accruals, prepayments, bad debts and allowance changes before finding profit.

Revenue $50,000; opening inventory $5,000; purchases $30,000; closing inventory $7,000; expenses $12,000. Cost of sales $28,000, gross profit $22,000, profit $10,000.
Check: Using revenue $50,000, opening inventory $5,000, purchases $30,000 and closing inventory $7,000, gross profit?
  1. $10,000
  2. $22,000
  3. $18,000
  4. $28,000

Answer B: Cost of sales = 5,000 + 30,000 − 7,000 = 28,000. Gross profit = 50,000 − 28,000 = 22,000.

Check: Owner takes resale goods costing $150 under a periodic inventory system. Entry?
  1. Debit expense; credit sales $150
  2. Debit bank; credit capital $150
  3. Debit purchases; credit drawings $150
  4. Debit drawings; credit purchases $150

Answer D: Remove the private goods from business purchases and charge drawings at cost.

5.1 · Financial position and drawings

Closing capital = opening capital + additional capital + profit − drawings.

Present non-current assets at carrying amount, then current assets, current liabilities and non-current liabilities. Intangible assets have no physical form, for example a purchased licence. Net assets equal owner capital. Goods withdrawn by the owner are recorded at cost: debit drawings and credit purchases under the periodic system. Private expenses paid by the business are drawings, not business expenses. Apply all year-end adjustments consistently across profit and position.

Opening capital $20,000 + profit $6,000 − drawings $4,000 gives closing capital $22,000. Goods drawings of $300 reduce purchases and increase drawings by $300.
Check: Opening capital $20,000, profit $6,000 and drawings $4,000; no other capital. Closing capital?
  1. $18,000
  2. $26,000
  3. $22,000
  4. $30,000

Answer C: 20,000 + 6,000 − 4,000 = 22,000. Drawings reduce the owner claim, not profit.

5.1 · Preparing a financial-position statement

Use a dated heading and reconcile net assets to owner funds.

Present non-current assets at cost less accumulated depreciation. Add current assets including inventory, net receivables, prepayments and bank debit balances. Deduct current liabilities such as payables, accruals and overdrafts. Show non-current borrowing separately. Reconcile the result to adjusted capital. A service business may have no resale inventory; a manufacturer can have three inventory categories. Never count an overdraft as a negative current asset and also as a liability.

Equipment cost $15,000 less depreciation $3,000 = $12,000. Current assets $8,000, current liabilities $4,000 and long-term loan $5,000. Net assets = 12,000 + 8,000 − 4,000 − 5,000 = $11,000.
Check: Using carrying equipment $12,000, current assets $8,000, current liabilities $4,000 and long-term loan $5,000, equity?
  1. $21,000
  2. $16,000
  3. $29,000
  4. $11,000

Answer D: Deduct both classes of liability from assets: 12,000 + 8,000 − 4,000 − 5,000.

5.2 · Partnership agreement and appropriation

Residual profit = profit + interest on drawings − salaries − interest on capital.

Partners can combine finance and expertise but share control, profit and normally unlimited liability. The agreement sets profit shares, salaries, interest on capital, drawings and loans. Partner loan interest is a business expense before appropriation. Partner salaries and capital interest divide profit rather than create operating expenses. Add interest on drawings to the amount available, deduct agreed allocations, and share the residual profit or loss in the agreed ratio.

Profit after loan interest $30,000. Salaries $6,000 and interest on capital $4,000; no interest on drawings. Residual $20,000 shared 3:2 gives $12,000 and $8,000.
Check: Profit $30,000, salaries $6,000, interest on capital $4,000; no drawings interest. Residual profit?
  1. $20,000
  2. $40,000
  3. $24,000
  4. $26,000

Answer A: Deduct the appropriations: 30,000 − 6,000 − 4,000 = 20,000.

Check: Interest on a partner loan is normally:
  1. An expense before appropriation
  2. A share of residual profit
  3. Drawings
  4. A reduction in sales

Answer A: Loan interest is a finance expense; interest on capital is an appropriation.

5.2 · Partners capital and current accounts

Current accounts record allocations and drawings when capital is fixed.

Fixed capital accounts record permanent investment and withdrawals of permanent capital. Current accounts receive salary, interest on capital and profit share as credits; drawings, interest on drawings and loss shares as debits. A debit current balance means the partner has taken more than credited allocations. Present partners funds consistently in the statement of financial position; partner loans remain liabilities. Prepare the underlying profit or loss with the same adjustments as a sole trader.

Current account credit $1,000 + salary $3,000 + profit share $5,000 − drawings $7,000 gives closing credit $2,000.
Check: Current credit balance $1,000, salary $3,000, profit share $5,000 and drawings $7,000. Closing balance?
  1. $8,000 credit
  2. $2,000 credit
  3. $2,000 debit
  4. $16,000 credit

Answer B: Credits 1,000 + 3,000 + 5,000 less debit drawings 7,000 give 2,000 credit.

5.2 · Allocating a partnership loss

Agreed appropriations can leave a residual loss.

Apply the agreement rather than assuming no allocations when profit is low. Partner salaries and interest on capital may exceed available profit, producing a residual loss divided by the profit-sharing ratio. Interest on drawings increases the available amount; partner loan interest is already an expense. Keep loan accounts separate from fixed capital and current accounts. A fixed-capital system and a fluctuating-capital system present owner movements differently.

Profit $4,000; salaries $6,000 and capital interest $2,000; no drawings interest. Residual is a $4,000 loss. Equal partners each take a $2,000 residual debit.
Check: Profit $4,000, salaries $6,000 and capital interest $2,000. Residual result?
  1. $12,000 profit
  2. $0
  3. $4,000 loss
  4. $4,000 profit

Answer C: 4,000 − 6,000 − 2,000 = −4,000.

5.3 · Company finance and limited liability

Equity is shareholder finance; a debenture is borrowing.

A company is legally separate from its owners. Limited liability normally limits shareholder loss to their investment and unpaid share commitments. Incorporation can improve access to capital but adds reporting and administration. Ordinary shares carry ownership rights with variable dividends; preference shares have stated priority rights. Debentures are loans with interest obligations. Issued capital is allocated to shareholders; called-up capital is the amount requested; paid-up capital is the amount actually paid. Companies may trade, provide services or manufacture.

10,000 shares of $1 each are issued, $0.80 called and $0.75 paid: issued $10,000, called-up $8,000, paid-up $7,500.
Check: 10,000 $1 shares are issued; $0.80 called and $0.75 paid per share. Paid-up capital?
  1. $8,000
  2. $10,000
  3. $500
  4. $7,500

Answer D: Paid-up capital is the amount paid: 10,000 × 0.75 = 7,500.

Check: A transfer from retained earnings to general reserve changes total equity by:
  1. Twice the transfer
  2. $0
  3. An increase equal to transfer
  4. A decrease equal to transfer

Answer B: Both accounts are equity components; the transfer only reclassifies equity.

5.3 · Company statements and changes in equity

Closing retained earnings = opening retained earnings + profit − dividends − transfers to reserves.

Prepare company profit or loss after expenses including loan interest, with standard year-end adjustments. Dividends are distributions, not operating expenses. A statement of changes in equity explains changes in share capital, reserves and retained earnings. A transfer to general reserve changes the composition of equity but not total equity. Present share capital, general reserve and retained earnings as equity; present debentures as a liability.

Opening retained earnings $12,000 + profit $8,000 − dividends $3,000 − transfer to reserve $2,000 = closing retained earnings $15,000. The reserve transfer leaves total equity unchanged.
Check: Opening retained earnings $12,000, profit $8,000, dividends $3,000, reserve transfer $2,000. Closing retained earnings?
  1. $15,000
  2. $17,000
  3. $19,000
  4. $25,000

Answer A: 12,000 + 8,000 − 3,000 − 2,000 = 15,000. The reserve grows separately by 2,000.

5.3 · Preparing a statement of changes in equity

Separate each equity column and explain movements.

Use columns for ordinary or preference share capital where relevant, general reserve, retained earnings and total equity. Begin with opening balances. New share issues increase the share-capital column and total equity. Profit increases retained earnings; dividends reduce them. Transfers between retained earnings and general reserve appear as equal opposite movements and leave total unchanged. Closing column totals feed the statement of financial position.

Opening equity $50,000 + new shares $10,000 + profit $8,000 − dividends $3,000 = closing equity $65,000. A $2,000 reserve transfer does not change $65,000.
Check: Opening equity $50,000, shares issued $10,000, profit $8,000 and dividends $3,000. Closing equity?
  1. $55,000
  2. $65,000
  3. $69,000
  4. $63,000

Answer B: 50,000 + 10,000 + 8,000 − 3,000 = 65,000; internal reserve transfers have no total effect.

5.4 · Manufacturing costs and work in progress

Production cost = prime cost + factory overheads + opening WIP − closing WIP.

Direct materials used equal opening raw materials plus purchases and carriage inwards less closing raw materials. Add direct labour and direct expenses to obtain prime cost. Add factory overheads such as indirect wages, factory rent and depreciation. Adjust for work in progress to find completed production cost. Transfer completed production to the trading section; selling and office costs belong in profit or loss, not factory cost. Statements of position include raw materials, WIP, finished goods and adjusted non-current assets.

Materials $18,000 + direct labour $12,000 + direct expenses $2,000 = prime cost $32,000. Overheads $9,000, opening WIP $4,000 and closing WIP $5,000 give production cost $40,000.
Check: Prime cost $32,000, overheads $9,000, opening WIP $4,000, closing WIP $5,000. Production cost?
  1. $50,000
  2. $32,000
  3. $40,000
  4. $42,000

Answer C: 32,000 + 9,000 + 4,000 − 5,000 = 40,000.

Check: Which cost belongs in factory overheads?
  1. Office stationery only
  2. Owner drawings
  3. Factory machinery depreciation
  4. Sales staff commission

Answer C: Factory depreciation supports production indirectly.

5.4 · From production cost to finished-goods profit

Completed production enters the cost of goods available for sale.

A manufacturing account calculates completed output cost. In the trading section, add opening finished goods and completed production, then deduct closing finished goods to calculate cost of sales. Do not use closing raw materials or WIP a second time here. Factory overhead adjustments belong in production; office and selling costs belong after gross profit. In financial position include raw materials, WIP and finished goods as separate inventory components or an appropriate total.

Opening finished goods $4,000 + completed production $39,000 − closing finished goods $6,000 = cost of sales $37,000. Revenue $60,000 gives gross profit $23,000.
Check: Opening finished goods $4,000, production $39,000, closing finished goods $6,000. Cost of sales?
  1. $41,000
  2. $49,000
  3. $39,000
  4. $37,000

Answer D: 4,000 + 39,000 − 6,000 = 37,000.

5.5 · Clubs cash and subscriptions

Subscription income = receipts + closing arrears − opening arrears + opening advances − closing advances.

A receipts and payments account summarises cash and bank movements, including capital items, without matching periods. An income and expenditure account measures the period surplus or deficit, excludes capital spending and includes non-cash adjustments. Subscription arrears are receivables; subscriptions in advance are liabilities. Prepare a separate trading account for activities such as refreshments, transferring its profit to income and expenditure.

Subscriptions received $5,000; opening arrears $300; closing arrears $500; opening advance $100; closing advance $200. Income = 5,000 + 500 − 300 + 100 − 200 = $5,100.
Check: Using receipts $5,000, opening/closing arrears $300/$500 and opening/closing advances $100/$200, income?
  1. $5,100
  2. $4,900
  3. $5,500
  4. $5,000

Answer A: Match the income to the period: 5,000 + 500 − 300 + 100 − 200 = 5,100.

Check: Which item belongs in receipts and payments but is not an expense in income and expenditure?
  1. Annual depreciation
  2. Accrued electricity only
  3. Increase in subscription arrears only
  4. Purchase of equipment

Answer D: Capital purchases are cash payments; depreciation is the period expense.

5.5 · Club statements and accumulated fund

Accumulated fund = assets − liabilities.

The accumulated fund is a club equivalent of capital. Build opening and closing statements of financial position from adjusted assets and liabilities. A surplus increases the accumulated fund; a deficit reduces it. Treat equipment purchases as assets with depreciation in income and expenditure, and apply accruals, prepayments and receivable adjustments. A refreshment activity profit is sales less its cost of sales and related expenses, not total cash takings.

Opening fund $14,000 and surplus $2,500, with no other fund movements, give closing fund $16,500.
Check: Club assets $20,000 and liabilities $3,500. Accumulated fund?
  1. $3,500
  2. $16,500
  3. $23,500
  4. $20,000

Answer B: Deduct obligations from resources: 20,000 − 3,500 = 16,500.

5.5 · Club refreshments and annual surplus

Activity profit, not activity receipts, feeds the annual result.

Prepare an activity trading account for a club shop or refreshments: sales less cost of sales gives gross profit, then deduct activity-specific expenses. Transfer the resulting profit to income and expenditure. Add earned subscriptions and other period income, then deduct period expenses including depreciation. Exclude capital spending and loan receipts. A deficit reduces the accumulated fund. A receipts-and-payments closing balance is cash, not accumulated fund.

Refreshment sales $8,000, opening inventory $500, purchases $5,000 and closing inventory $700. Cost of sales $4,800 and gross profit $3,200. Wages $1,000 leave activity profit $2,200.
Check: Using sales $8,000, opening inventory $500, purchases $5,000, closing inventory $700 and wages $1,000, activity profit?
  1. $1,800
  2. $8,000
  3. $2,200
  4. $3,200

Answer C: 8,000 − (500 + 5,000 − 700) − 1,000 = 2,200.

5.6 · Incomplete records and statements of affairs

Profit = closing capital − opening capital + drawings − additional capital.

A small trader may retain invoices and cash records without a full double-entry ledger because it seems cheaper, but incomplete records weaken control and reliability. Statements of affairs estimate opening and closing capital from assets less liabilities. Adjust the capital change for owner transactions to derive profit. Reconstruct missing sales or purchases with receivable and payable relationships and then prepare adjusted trading statements. This syllabus application concerns sole-trader trading businesses.

Opening capital $15,000, closing capital $22,000, drawings $4,000, additional capital $3,000. Profit = 22,000 − 15,000 + 4,000 − 3,000 = $8,000.
Check: Using capital opening $15,000/closing $22,000, drawings $4,000 and new capital $3,000, profit?
  1. $14,000
  2. $6,000
  3. $7,000
  4. $8,000

Answer D: Remove new investment and restore drawings: 22,000 − 15,000 + 4,000 − 3,000 = 8,000.

Check: Opening receivables $2,000, closing $3,000 and receipts $9,000; no other items. Credit sales?
  1. $10,000
  2. $8,000
  3. $14,000
  4. $6,000

Answer A: Credit sales = closing 3,000 + receipts 9,000 − opening 2,000 = 10,000.

5.6 · Reconstructing sales, purchases and inventory

Mark-up uses cost; margin uses revenue.

For simple receivables with no adjustments, credit sales equal closing receivables plus customer receipts less opening receivables. Include returns, discounts and bad debts when given. Reconstruct purchases similarly using the payables account. A 25% mark-up means revenue is 125% of cost; it corresponds to a 20% margin. Inventory turnover can reconstruct cost of sales from average inventory. Add cash sales separately to reconstructed credit sales.

Cost of sales $24,000 and mark-up 25% give gross profit $6,000 and revenue $30,000. Margin = 6,000 ÷ 30,000 = 20%.
Check: Cost of sales $24,000 with 25% mark-up gives revenue:
  1. $30,000
  2. $32,000
  3. $18,000
  4. $6,000

Answer A: Revenue = cost × 1.25 = 24,000 × 1.25 = 30,000.

5.6 · Reconstructing purchases with adjustments

Use a payables control working even when full records are missing.

Credit purchases = closing payables − opening payables + supplier payments + purchases returns + discount received + contra reductions, after allowing for any other specified entries. Add cash purchases only when finding total purchases. Check that all values relate to credit suppliers. Reconstruct revenue with receivables in the same way, adding returns, discounts and debts written off as reductions that must be restored when solving for sales.

Opening payables $2,000, closing $2,700, payments $9,000, returns $300 and discount $100. Credit purchases = 2,700 − 2,000 + 9,000 + 300 + 100 = $10,100.
Check: Using opening payables $2,000, closing $2,700, payments $9,000, returns $300 and discount $100, credit purchases?
  1. $14,100
  2. $10,100
  3. $9,300
  4. $8,900

Answer B: Rearrange the payables equation: closing − opening + payments + returns + discount.

6. Financial analysis

6.1 · Profitability ratios

Gross margin = gross profit/revenue ×100; mark-up = gross profit/cost of sales ×100.

Profit margin is profit for the year divided by revenue times 100. ROCE uses profit before interest divided by capital employed times 100. For the specified company formula, capital employed is issued shares plus reserves plus non-current liabilities. Keep ratio inputs consistent with the Cambridge formula sheet rather than mixing alternative textbook definitions. Profitability measures return and cost control, not immediate cash availability.

Revenue $100,000; cost of sales $60,000; profit $10,000. Gross margin 40%, mark-up 66.67%, profit margin 10%. Profit before interest $12,000 and capital employed $80,000 give ROCE 15%.
Check: Profit before interest $12,000 and capital employed $80,000. ROCE?
  1. 10%
  2. 66.67%
  3. 15%
  4. 6.67%

Answer C: 12,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 15%. Use profit before interest.

Check: Cost of sales $40,000 and average inventory $8,000. Inventory turnover?
  1. 8 times
  2. 5 times
  3. 20%
  4. 50 days

Answer B: 40,000 ÷ 8,000 = 5 times.

6.1 · Liquidity ratios

Current ratio = current assets/current liabilities; acid test excludes inventory.

Present liquidity ratios as x:1. The acid test removes inventory because inventory is often slower to turn into cash. A high ratio may reflect idle funds or slow receivables, not efficient management. A low ratio may indicate pressure, but payment patterns and industry context matter. Use total current liabilities, and avoid treating all non-current liabilities as immediate obligations.

Current assets $30,000 include inventory $12,000; current liabilities $10,000. Current ratio 3:1, acid test 1.8:1.
Check: Current assets $30,000 including inventory $12,000; current liabilities $10,000. Acid test?
  1. 3:1
  2. 1.2:1
  3. 0.6:1
  4. 1.8:1

Answer D: (30,000 − 12,000) ÷ 10,000 = 1.8:1.

6.1 · Efficiency ratios

Inventory turnover times = cost of sales/average inventory.

Average inventory is normally (opening + closing inventory)/2. Inventory days = average inventory/cost of sales ×365. Receivables days = trade receivables/credit sales ×365; payables days = trade payables/credit purchases ×365. Use the credit totals specified, not total revenue or cash purchases. Report days and times distinctly. Longer inventory or receivable periods can tie up cash; longer payable periods can harm supplier relationships.

Cost of sales $73,000, average inventory $10,000: turnover 7.3 times and inventory days 50. Receivables $6,000 and annual credit sales $73,000 give 30 days.
Check: Receivables $6,000; annual credit sales $73,000. Receivables days using 365?
  1. 30 days
  2. 12.17 days
  3. 50 days
  4. 7.3 days

Answer A: 6,000 ÷ 73,000 × 365 = 30 days.

6.1 · Turnover periods and capital employed

Use the specified numerator and denominator, with units.

Inventory days use average inventory divided by cost of sales times 365. Receivable days use closing trade receivables divided by credit sales times 365. Payable days use trade payables divided by credit purchases times 365. ROCE uses profit before interest and capital employed. For the specified company formula, add issued shares, reserves and non-current liabilities; do not silently exclude debentures. A zero denominator means the ratio cannot be calculated meaningfully.

Payables $4,000 and annual credit purchases $73,000 give 20 days. Shares $40,000 + reserves $15,000 + non-current borrowing $5,000 give capital employed $60,000.
Check: Payables $4,000 and annual credit purchases $73,000 give payables days:
  1. 5 days
  2. 20 days
  3. 18.25 days
  4. 30 days

Answer B: 4,000 ÷ 73,000 × 365 = 20 days.

6.2 · Interpreting results and recommending action

State the change, explain a plausible cause, then evaluate an action.

Compare both absolute results and ratios across years. Lower selling prices, higher purchase costs or understated closing inventory can reduce gross margin. Expense growth can reduce profit margin even when gross margin is stable. Cash differs from profit because of credit transactions, inventory changes, depreciation, drawings, loans and capital purchases. To improve liquidity consider credit control, inventory management or suitable long-term finance, and explain the trade-off instead of assuming a single action is always best.

Gross margin is unchanged at 40% but profit margin falls from 12% to 8%. Investigate operating expenses rather than claiming the cost-of-sales percentage increased.
Check: Gross margin is unchanged while profit margin falls. A plausible explanation is:
  1. Drawings are an operating expense
  2. All sales were cash sales
  3. Operating expenses rose relative to revenue
  4. Cost of sales percentage necessarily rose

Answer C: A larger operating expense share reduces profit margin after gross profit; drawings do not enter profit.

Check: Which action can improve collection without directly increasing borrowing?
  1. Increase drawings
  2. Ignore credit limits
  3. Follow up overdue customer balances
  4. Buy more equipment for cash

Answer C: Credit control can accelerate receipts, although customer relationships also matter.

6.2 · Working-capital decisions

Working capital = current assets − current liabilities.

Slow-moving inventory ties up funds; reduce unnecessary stock while avoiding shortages. Offer suitable credit terms and follow up receivables without driving away reliable customers. Negotiate supplier terms rather than simply delaying payment. Long-term finance can replace pressure from a short-term overdraft, but creates interest and repayment obligations. Compare a recommendation with the specific ratios, cash pattern and non-financial context.

Current assets $25,000 and current liabilities $18,000 give working capital $7,000. A bank loan repayable after five years raises cash and non-current liabilities, improving working capital initially while creating future commitments.
Check: Current assets $25,000 and current liabilities $18,000. Working capital?
  1. $43,000
  2. $18,000
  3. $25,000
  4. $7,000

Answer D: 25,000 − 18,000 = 7,000.

6.3 · Comparing different businesses

Compare like with like and explain the limits.

Ratios can differ because of industry, business size, product mix, age of assets, credit policy, accounting policies and financial year-end. One business with older depreciated assets may show higher ROCE without better operations. Similar ratios can conceal very different absolute scale and risk. Make a supported judgement with comparable periods and policies; do not rank businesses by one ratio alone.

A supermarket collects mostly cash while a wholesaler sells on 60-day credit. Comparing their receivables days without explaining business models is misleading.
Check: Which factor can weaken comparison of ROCE between two businesses?
  1. Different asset ages and depreciation policies
  2. Both ratios use percentages
  3. Both businesses have names
  4. Both publish statements

Answer A: Older or differently depreciated asset bases can change the denominator without equivalent differences in performance.

Check: A fair comparison should consider:
  1. Only one ratio
  2. Only the company name
  3. No non-financial information
  4. Accounting policies and business activities

Answer D: Comparable policies and operating contexts are needed for a meaningful judgement.

6.4 · Who uses accounting information

Different users ask different financial questions.

Owners assess profit and returns; managers plan and control; employees assess job security and wage prospects. Banks, lenders and investors consider returns and repayment risk. Suppliers assess whether credit will be paid; customers consider continuity of supply. Governments and tax authorities need reported results; club members assess stewardship. Public and environmental groups may seek evidence of wider impacts that conventional statements do not fully capture.

A supplier deciding whether to grant 30-day credit examines liquidity, payment history and amounts already owed, rather than revenue alone.
Check: A supplier considering short-term trade credit is most directly interested in:
  1. Owner drawings as an expense
  2. Ability to pay debts when due
  3. Only the business logo
  4. Only gross sales volume

Answer B: Liquidity and payment behaviour help assess settlement risk.

Check: Club members mainly use accounts to assess:
  1. Stewardship of club resources
  2. Only supplier tax rates
  3. Only a customer credit limit
  4. Personal bank balances of all staff

Answer A: Members need to see how subscriptions and other resources were used.

6.5 · Limits of financial statements

Financial figures are useful evidence, not a complete picture.

Historical costs can become outdated in changing prices. Different depreciation, inventory and other policies can limit comparability. Statements do not fully measure workforce skill, management quality, location, customer loyalty or economic conditions. Published information also describes a past period. Combine financial analysis with relevant non-financial information and explain uncertainty in recommendations.

Two shops report the same profit; one loses its lease next month. Financial statements alone may not reveal the operational risk of relocation.
Check: Which important factor is not fully captured in conventional financial statements?
  1. Recorded trade payables
  2. Recorded equipment cost
  3. Workforce skill and customer loyalty
  4. Recorded bank balance

Answer C: Many valuable non-financial factors cannot be measured reliably as accounting amounts.

Check: Historic cost can limit usefulness because:
  1. It includes every non-financial benefit
  2. Recorded costs may differ from current values
  3. Every asset must be recorded at market value
  4. It removes all subjectivity

Answer B: Original costs can become less representative as prices change.

7. Concepts, ethics and technology

7.1 · Accounting concepts in practice

Separate the entity, recognise earned income and match period costs.

Business entity separates owner and business transactions. Duality records the two-sided effect. Going concern assumes continuation where appropriate; historical cost records original transaction amounts. Matching or accruals charges expenses against related revenue; realisation recognises earned revenue rather than waiting for cash. Prudence avoids overstating income and assets or understating liabilities. Consistency uses comparable policies; materiality considers significance. Money measurement limits records to reliably measurable money amounts.

The owner pays a personal bill from business bank: drawings, not expense (entity). Charge depreciation consistently (consistency). Record unsold damaged inventory at lower NRV (prudence).
Check: Recording an owner private holiday as drawings applies which concept?
  1. Going concern
  2. Money measurement only
  3. Realisation
  4. Business entity

Answer D: The business is treated separately from the owner; personal consumption is drawings.

Check: Recognising an unpaid electricity expense in its correct period applies:
  1. Drawings
  2. Trade discount
  3. Matching / accruals
  4. Cash basis only

Answer C: The expense belongs to the period of consumption, irrespective of payment.

7.1 · Applying all ten accounting concepts

Explain the concept through the transaction, not only its definition.

Entity: owner spending is drawings. Duality: a loan increases bank and liabilities. Going concern: allocate asset cost over expected use when continuation is appropriate. Historic cost: retain original transaction cost as the measurement base. Matching: recognise unpaid period expenses. Realisation: recognise earned credit sales. Consistency: use comparable depreciation policies. Prudence: write down damaged inventory when NRV is lower. Materiality: judge whether an omission can influence decisions. Money measurement: do not invent an asset value for staff morale. A justified policy change is possible; consistency does not mean never correcting a poor policy.

A credit sale is earned on delivery although cash comes next month: realisation. An unpaid electricity bill for the same month is included: matching. Ignoring a large payable is material because it can affect lending decisions.
Check: Recognising an earned credit sale before cash collection most directly illustrates:
  1. Realisation
  2. Cash accounting
  3. Drawings
  4. Imprest

Answer A: Income is recognised when earned, not simply when cash arrives.

7.2 · Ethics and professional judgement

Be honest, unbiased, competent, confidential and professionally responsible.

Integrity means truthful records. Objectivity means avoiding bias and conflicts. Competence and due care require current knowledge and careful work. Confidentiality restricts improper disclosure or use of information. Professional behaviour requires responsible conduct and compliance with obligations. An ethical framework protects stakeholders who rely on reports; pressure from an owner does not justify inventing sales or hiding liabilities. Document concerns and use appropriate reporting channels.

A manager asks to omit an unpaid invoice to increase profit. The accountant should retain the liability and expense where appropriate, explain the impact and escalate the pressure through proper channels.
Check: An accountant refuses to hide a liability to improve reported profit. Which principle is most directly demonstrated?
  1. Imprest
  2. Integrity
  3. Inventory turnover
  4. Trade discount

Answer B: Truthful reporting demonstrates integrity and protects people relying on the accounts.

Check: Not disclosing customer financial records without proper authority demonstrates:
  1. Profit margin
  2. Prime cost
  3. Realisation only
  4. Confidentiality

Answer D: Confidentiality protects entrusted information from improper disclosure.

7.2 · Ethical conflicts and stakeholders

Pressure to improve a result does not justify a misleading record.

Objectivity requires independent judgement when a relative is a supplier. Competence and due care require checking calculations and seeking help when work exceeds expertise. Confidentiality prohibits using entrusted information for private gain as well as improper disclosure. Professional behaviour requires responsible conduct and respect for obligations. Integrity prevents intentional misstatement. These principles help owners, employees, lenders, suppliers and the wider community trust accounting information.

An accountant discovers a spreadsheet formula omitted a liability. Due care requires investigating and correcting it. If a manager insists it remain omitted, integrity and objectivity require resisting that pressure and escalating appropriately.
Check: An accountant checks unfamiliar accounting work with a qualified colleague. This best demonstrates:
  1. Capital expenditure
  2. Credit control
  3. Professional competence and due care
  4. A higher gross margin

Answer C: Recognising limits and obtaining competent assistance supports careful professional work.

7.3 · Digital accounting and sustainable storage

Automation needs reliable inputs, controlled access and recoverable records.

Digital tools can capture invoices, post journals, calculate totals and produce reports quickly. Human checks remain essential for classification and unusual transactions. Paper storage is accessible without software but vulnerable to fire and loss. Local devices allow control but need protected backups; cloud services support remote access but depend on connectivity and provider security. Use access permissions, encryption where appropriate, tested backups and retention schedules. Sustainable storage reduces unnecessary copies and resource use without destroying records that must be retained.

A business keeps encrypted working records plus a separately protected backup and tests restoration. It removes redundant copies under an agreed retention policy while retaining required evidence.
Check: Which control best protects against loss of the only accounting device?
  1. A second folder on the same device only
  2. Sharing every password
  3. Deleting all source invoices
  4. A separately stored backup that is tested

Answer D: A separate, recoverable backup survives device loss; a folder on the same device may be lost with it.

Check: A weakness of cloud-only accounting access is:
  1. Dependence on connectivity and the provider
  2. It can never be backed up
  3. It eliminates human errors
  4. It requires no access control

Answer A: Cloud services have availability and provider risks even when they offer useful remote access.

7.3 · Comparing storage systems

Choose recoverability, access and retention together.

Paper records do not need electricity to read but need physical space and protection from fire, water and unauthorised viewing. Local devices give direct control but can fail or be stolen. Cloud systems support remote access and shared working but introduce provider, internet and permission risks. Backups should be separate and restore-tested; synchronisation alone can copy accidental deletion. Sustainable retention avoids unnecessary duplication while preserving necessary records and safe disposal. No specific accounting software is required to understand these principles.

A cloud sync immediately copies a mistaken deletion to every device. A versioned backup with a tested restore process can recover the record; merely having two synced folders might not.
Check: Why is synchronisation alone not a complete backup strategy?
  1. It may replicate deletion or corruption
  2. It cannot use the internet
  3. It always prints a paper copy
  4. It prevents all unauthorised access

Answer A: Synchronisation can reproduce the same mistake everywhere; recoverable versions and restore tests matter.

For calculations, show a formula, substituted figures, result and unit. For accounts, use dates, names, debit/credit sides and balances. For analysis, connect a numerical change with a possible cause. For evaluation, weigh an action and its limitation before recommending it.
